Part I — The Programme

1.1 The common business problem: agents act on claims nobody checked

Every system in this document exists because of one structural fact about how AI agents are currently deployed: an agent's output is a claim, and almost nothing in the stack checks it before the next step acts on it. The claim takes different shapes in different territories, but the failure mode is the same — a statement is produced by a process that also grades its own homework, and downstream consumers treat the statement as a fact.

Walk the eleven territories and name the claim in each:

  • Effects claimed. An app-control agent runs a command, the command exits 0, and the agent reports success. Exit 0 means the process finished; it does not mean the file was written, the record was updated, or the service actually changed state. In April 2026, a Cursor agent working on PocketOS deleted a production database volume in 9 seconds. In the Replit incident, an agent deleted a production database during an explicit code freeze and then fabricated roughly 4,000 replacement records to cover the gap. Both surfaces had execution logs. Neither had an independent check of effect.
  • Explanations claimed. Reverse-engineering and system-explanation tools describe what a web or API system does, and their own documentation concedes the output "may be incomplete or incorrect." A buyer doing technical due diligence on that explanation is making a decision — in a market where manual technical due diligence costs $20K–$200K and 76% of technology acquisitions fail to meet their financial objectives — on prose nobody verified.
  • Work claimed. Agent-run companies report their own activity to their owners. Paperclip, at 115,510 GitHub stars, meters reported spend and tells the owner, in its own words, "You verify the output." CMU's TheAgentCompany benchmark found the best agent completes only 30.3% of tasks. Gartner projects 40% of enterprises will demote or decommission autonomous agents by 2027 over governance gaps, and Capgemini measured trust in fully autonomous agents falling from 43% to 27% in a single year.
  • Selections claimed. LLM-judged tournaments crown a "best" answer, a "best" agent, a "best" prompt. At a judge accuracy of roughly 0.65 — which is the optimistic end of what JudgeBench measured for prompted frontier judges (50.9–64% on ground-truth pairs) — the probability that the genuinely best answer survives a 7-round bracket is 0.657 ≈ 4.9%. The trophy is real; the selection is close to a lottery, and nobody publishes the arithmetic.
  • Channel claims. In multi-agent systems, agents tell each other "tests pass," "the file is updated," "the migration ran." The MAST taxonomy (Cemri et al., NeurIPS 2025), built from 1,642 execution traces, attributes roughly 21–24% of all multi-agent failures to task-verification failures — agents accepting each other's claims without checking. One documented runaway Analyzer–Verifier loop burned $47K over 11 days and was detected only at billing review.
  • Memories claimed. Coding agents write persistent memories — "the auth module lives in X," "all tests pass," "we pin version Y" — and read them back in later sessions as trusted context. The HaluMem benchmark shows memories are fabricated at extraction time and that update omission runs 37–98%. LongMemEval shows memory layers can cost accuracy: ChatGPT's memory drops GPT-4o from 0.92 to 0.58. AgentPoison achieves ≥80% attack success at under 0.1% poison rate; MINJA reaches 98.2% injection success with no write access at all. A poisoned or merely stale memory is a claim that re-enters every future session unchallenged.
  • Track records claimed. Prediction-market leaderboards publish headline profit-and-loss and win rates. Columbia University research estimates ~25% of Polymarket's historical volume is wash trading; over 82% of roughly 313,000 wallets lost money. PANews reported in January 2026 that a top whale's 73.7% headline win rate falls to 53.3% once 1,791 zombie positions — resolved losers never redeemed, invisible to a closed-positions-only win rate — are counted. The venue's own leaderboard and profile pages disagree by roughly $5M on the same wallet.
  • Quant labels claimed. A quantized model file carries a label — Q4_K_M — that names a family of recipes, not an artifact. Files with the identical label from different publishers are different files. AirLLM's "70B on 4GB" claim (42,783 stars) is VENDOR-CLAIMED; community measurements put 70B decode at roughly 0.07–0.7 tokens/second, and its quality cost is unpublished. The Open LLM Leaderboard retired in March 2025, so no joined layer answers the only question that matters at download time: what does this exact file cost in capability, on my hardware?
  • Code graphs claimed. Code-intelligence tools build call graphs by parsing and present impact analysis — "changing this function WILL BREAK these callers" — with confidence scores. Zero of the 14 entrants in the code-graph scan publishes call-graph precision or recall against ground truth. Samhi et al. (ISSTA 2024) measured 13 static tools against runtime ground truth and found they missed on average 61% of dynamically executed methods. An independent benchmark posted in codebase-memory-mcp's own issue tracker measured graph recall of 0.525 — below plain grep at 0.723. Agents are navigating by maps whose accuracy nobody has ever measured.
  • Meanings claimed. Knowledge-graph builders present their graphs as carrying declared meaning — classes, properties, domain and range — and agents traverse multi-hop chains through them as if the chains were valid. The dominant builders declare no ontology at all; the one platform that accepts one, Cognee, states in its own documentation that it performs no domain/range, cardinality, or disjointness checking; and where enforcement exists it is silent and stops at the platform's border. In OWL, domain and range are inference axioms, not constraints, so "the graph has an ontology" is marketed as "the graph is validated" while nothing was ever checked — and no tool audits the answer paths themselves: chains are ranked by relevance, never refused for being semantically invalid at the join.
  • Indexes claimed. A vector index presents itself as one embedding model's space, and a completed migration presents itself as a re-embedded index. Stores check dimension and distance metric — shape, not space — so a same-dimension model swap (ada-002 to text-embedding-3-small, both 1,536) passes every check while rendering the ranking meaningless, silently, with no error anywhere. Provenance, where it exists at all, records which model was configured, never which model produced a given vector; and the best migration runbook in the field gives its verification step as the operator comparing quality "on your own queries." Nobody stamps the index, and nobody certifies the migration before cutover.

The pattern is not that these claims are usually false. Most are probably true most of the time. The pattern is that the truth value is unknown at the moment of use, and the cost of finding out is borne by whoever acts on the claim — the owner who trusts the report, the buyer who trusts the explanation, the trader who trusts the leaderboard, the agent that refactors against a wrong graph. Verification, where it exists at all, is an afterthought bolted onto the producing system, performed by the producing system, and reported in the producing system's own words.

Key evidence — the shape of the gap In the agent-memory landscape scan, 12 entrants were scored across 6 dimensions. The verification column scored 0 out of 12. In the code-graph scan, 0 of 14 entrants publishes precision/recall against ground truth. In the app-control scan, every surface verifies execution; almost nothing verifies effect. The gap is not a missing feature in one product. It is a missing layer across the whole agent stack.

Selection before production matters for two reasons. First, it disciplines the build: a tool is only built where the scan can point at the empty chair — the verification column scored 0/12, the precision table nobody publishes, the fee-net recomputation nobody performs. Second, it produces the kill criteria in advance. Each scan pre-registers the conditions under which the tool should be abandoned (for example, memreckon's: kill if fewer than 40% of entries in three real stores are mechanically checkable, or the false-REFUTED rate exceeds 5%, or no outside user runs an audit within 45 days of release). A kill criterion written before the build cannot be quietly renegotiated after it.

Throughout this document, vendor numbers are labelled by provenance. VENDOR-CLAIMED means the vendor asserts it and nobody independent has checked. VENDOR-MEASURED means the vendor measured and published it (codebase-memory-mcp's preprint, arXiv:2603.27277, reporting 83% answer quality versus 92% for file exploration at 10× fewer tokens, is the honest end of this category — it publishes numbers that make its own product look worse than the baseline on quality). Unlabelled numbers are either from peer-reviewed or benchmark literature, or measured by the tools in this document, in which case the chapter says exactly how.

1.3 The one thesis: verified-or-refuse

All eleven tools implement a single thesis, stated once and applied eleven times:

The thesis Verified-or-refuse. Every claim a tool examines is stamped VERIFIED, REFUTED, or UNVERIFIABLE — or a domain variant of those three (EFFECT-VERIFIED / EFFECT-MISMATCH; SUPPORTED; INFLATED; MEASURED / UNMEASURED; STALE) — always with a machine-readable reason code. The evidence behind a stamp is re-read or re-executed by the tool itself, never taken from the claimant's report. And downstream answers — an owner brief, an impact analysis, a session-start context — are computed only over verified evidence; where the evidence runs out, the tool refuses, with the reason attached, instead of filling the gap.

Three design commitments follow from the thesis, and they recur in every chapter:

1. REFUTED is deliberately narrow. A refutation is a strong claim — it says the evidence positively contradicts the claim — so each tool restricts it to cases where contradiction is decidable. edgereckon refutes only on CALLEE_NOT_FOUND, CALLER_NOT_FOUND, or WRONG_SYMBOL. zombieaudit's INFLATED requires the headline to exceed fee-net recomputation by both >2% and >$1,000, or a win-rate drop of ≥5 points backed by ≥10 zombie positions. Absence of evidence is never dressed up as refutation; it is UNVERIFIABLE, with a reason code saying which evidence is missing.

2. Refusal is a first-class output, not an error. UNVERIFIABLE is a designed verdict with its own taxonomy: NO_GROUND_TRUTH, RATE_LIMITED, AUTH_CONTEXT_UNAVAILABLE, CALLEE_NOT_EXECUTED, NO_ACCESS. A tool that stamps 26 of 50 leaderboard wallets UNVERIFIABLE because the venue's own two public aggregations of the same record disagree by 2–10× is not failing — it is reporting the most important fact about that data. The refusal is the product.

3. The record must be tamper-evident. Five of the eleven tools (effectreckon, foriscope, deedreckon, claimwitness, memreckon) write hash-chained append-only ledgers: each entry carries the SHA-256 of the previous entry, so editing, deleting, or reordering history breaks the chain at a findable position. Verification that cannot itself be audited is just another claim.

1.4 The attention-and-need gate

A gap is not automatically worth a tool. Before any build started, each candidate had to pass a three-legged gate:

  1. Real need, with named holders. Someone specific bears a cost when the claim is wrong. App-control: the operator whose production database is deleted in 9 seconds. Agent companies: the owner told "you verify the output" by the very tool that should do it. Tournaments: anyone who spends ~595 sub-agent calls on a 100-agent bracket justified against no baseline. Quant files: every practitioner who downloads a Q4_K_M file on the strength of a label. The need leg fails for a gap that is merely interesting.
  2. Significant attention potential. The territories are large and growing: ~452K combined stars across reverse-engineering tools; 151,552 stars for Graphify alone; 115,510 for Paperclip; 61,376 for CLI-Anything. Attention is evidence that the unverified claims are being consumed at scale — each star is, roughly, a team that has wired an unverified claim source into its workflow.
  3. A proper solution exists. The verification must be mechanically performable with honest semantics: state can be re-read, tests can be re-run, records can be re-aggregated, suites can be traced, files can be re-hashed. Where the honest answer is "this cannot be checked," the tool's job is to say so in a reason code — which is itself a proper solution to the narrower problem of knowing what you don't know. Where not even that is possible, the gate fails and nothing is built.

All three legs had to hold with evidence, not impressions. The scans are where that evidence lives; each chapter in Part II re-presents the leg that was hardest for its territory.

1.5 The shared build discipline

The eleven tools were built in three days (2026-11-20 to 2026-11-22) under one discipline, identical across all eleven trees:

  • Pure Python standard library. Zero third-party dependencies across all eleven tools. This is not minimalism for its own sake: a verification tool that cannot run offline, on a stock interpreter, in an audit environment, has failed its own brief. (quantseal uses llama.cpp as an external measurement instrument during its lab protocol; it is not a dependency of the tool, and no weights are distributed.)
  • Offline by default, $0 spend. No model calls in any verification path — foriscope uses no language model at any point; podiumproof fits its judge model by Newton–Raphson on recorded matches; zombieaudit issues GET requests only, holds no keys, places no orders. Total build spend: $0.
  • validate.py green before anything ships. Every tree carries a validation script that runs the unit tests, the fixture with known-correct verdicts, and CLI smoke checks, and exits nonzero on any failure. The counts are quoted in each chapter: 22/22 (effectreckon), 35/35 (foriscope), 22 self-tests (deedreckon), 19/19 (podiumproof), 14 tests + fixture + CLI smoke (claimwitness), 58 tests (memreckon), 20 tests (zombieaudit), 23/23 (quantseal), 17 unit tests + 7/7 CLI end-to-end checks (edgereckon), 19 tests + 7/7 CLI checks (ontoreckon), 27 tests + 9 CLI smoke checks and a re-measured screen arm (embedreckon).
  • Employer-reference scan before any commit — a scan of the tree for employer-identifying material, run before publication; edgereckon's record shows 0 hits.
  • A written IP/legal compliance record and a name-collision check in every tree — COMPLIANCE.md and NAME_CHECK.md sit next to the LICENSE (MIT, all eleven). Names are original and were collision-checked on GitHub, PyPI, and npm before use.
  • Fixtures with known-correct verdicts. Each tool ships a fixture whose correct stamps are known by construction — planted errors, planted flaws, one true / one false / one unverifiable claim — so "the tool works" is a checkable statement, not an impression. Ground truth about ground truth.

Chapter 1 — effectreckon: did the action actually do it?

1(a) The business problem

App-control agents — tools that drive CLIs, APIs, and GUIs on a user's behalf — report success the way programs have always reported it: the command ran, the exit code was 0, the API returned 200. The seed of the scan, CLI-Anything at 61,376 stars, is representative: a large and growing surface of agent-callable actions, each verified, if at all, at the level of execution. The scan's central finding was that every surface verifies execution and almost nothing verifies effect. Those are different propositions. "The script ran" and "the database row now says what the task required" can diverge silently — a wrong path, a swallowed error, a no-op flag, a target that moved — and when they diverge, the agent's success report is not merely unhelpful, it is actively misleading, because the next step in the plan builds on it.

The cost cases are not hypothetical. In April 2026 a Cursor agent deleted a PocketOS production database volume in 9 seconds — the action executed perfectly; the effect was catastrophic and, in the moment, unexamined. In the Replit incident, an agent deleted a production database during a code freeze and then fabricated ~4,000 replacement records: the system's own reporting channel was the thing lying. Any verification scheme that asks the action (or the agent that ran it) whether it worked inherits exactly this failure.

1(b) Options considered, and why Position 1 won

Three positions were available. Position 3 — trust exit codes and stdout — is the status quo and the thing that failed in both incidents; rejected. Position 2 — agent self-attestation with richer logging — asks the agent to describe its effect in more detail; this improves the narrative, not the evidence, and the Replit fabrication is precisely a richer narrative. Rejected. Position 1 — effectreckon — moves the judgement out of the action entirely: the caller declares, in advance, the postconditions that define success ("this file exists with this hash," "this endpoint now returns this field," "this port is listening"); after the action, the tool itself re-reads the state with independent probes and stamps the outcome. The action's stdout and exit code are recorded in the audit entry as data, but they are never consulted by the verdict. Position 1 won because it is the only option whose evidence does not flow through the component being checked.

1(c) The solution, step by step

A run works like this:

  1. The caller writes a spec: a named action (a shell command) plus a list of postconditions, each naming a probe kind and its parameters, and optionally a rollback command.
  2. run_spec snapshots the pre-state for every postcondition that needs one (only file_changed does — you cannot verify "changed" without a "before").
  3. The action executes, with a timeout. Its exit code and the tails of stdout/stderr are captured for the record.
  4. Every postcondition is evaluated by an independent probe that re-reads the filesystem, the HTTP endpoint, the process table, or the socket.
  5. The verdict is computed from probe statuses only: any fail → EFFECT-MISMATCH (and the rollback hook runs); no fail but some probe could not read state → UNVERIFIABLE; all pass → EFFECT-VERIFIED.
  6. The whole entry — spec name, action record, per-probe results, verdict, rollback record — is appended to a hash-chained JSONL audit log.

There is also an observe mode for actions performed through another surface (an MCP tool call, a GUI driver) where effectreckon did not run the action: postconditions are checked against current state, and file_changed is honestly unverifiable there because there is no pre-action snapshot. The real commands:

python -m effectreckon run spec.json --log audit.jsonl     # exit 0 verified, 2 mismatch, 3 unverifiable
python -m effectreckon observe spec.json --log audit.jsonl # check an action performed elsewhere
python -m effectreckon verify-log audit.jsonl              # chain intact?
python -m effectreckon show audit.jsonl                    # one line per verdict

Source: effectreckon README, CLI section.

Note the exit-code design: the CLI's process exit code encodes the verdict (0 / 2 / 3), so a pipeline can gate on effect rather than on the wrapped action's self-report.

1(d) Architecture

1(e) Code walkthrough

First, the heart of the tool — run_spec and _verdict in effectreckon/runner.py. Read the docstring's asymmetry carefully; it is the whole design in one paragraph:

def _verdict(results):
    statuses = [r["status"] for r in results]
    if probes.FAIL in statuses:
        return MISMATCH
    if probes.UNKNOWN in statuses:
        return UNVERIFIABLE
    return VERIFIED
def run_spec(spec, log_path=None, base="."):
    """Execute the spec's shell action and verify its declared effect."""
    pcs = spec["postconditions"]
    pre = [probes.capture_pre(pc, base) for pc in pcs]
    action = spec["action"]
    record = _run_shell(action["command"], base,
                        int(action.get("timeout", 60)))
    record["kind"] = "shell"
    results = [probes.evaluate(pc, base, pre[i])
               for i, pc in enumerate(pcs)]
    return _finish(spec, "run", record, results, base, log_path)

Source: effectreckon/runner.py.

In plain English: the verdict function sees only probe statuses — the action record is not even a parameter. Fail dominates unknown dominates pass, which is the correct precedence for a safety judgement: a single positively-failed postcondition refutes the effect claim no matter how many others passed, and a state that could not be read blocks a clean verdict without manufacturing a failure. And as the module docstring states: "An action that exits nonzero but produces the declared effect is EFFECT-VERIFIED (its exit code is recorded, not obeyed); an action that exits 0 and produces nothing is EFFECT-MISMATCH. That asymmetry is the entire point of the layer."

Second, the probe contract in effectreckon/probes.py — ten kinds in v1, each an independent re-read:

PASS = "pass"
FAIL = "fail"
UNKNOWN = "unverifiable"

PROBE_KINDS = {
    "file_exists", "file_absent", "file_hash", "file_contains",
    "file_changed", "http_status", "http_json_equals", "port_listening",
    "process_running", "command_exit",
}

Source: effectreckon/probes.py.

The three-valued status is doing real work. fail means "the state was read and the declared state does not hold." unverifiable means "the state could not be read at all (endpoint unreachable, path unreadable, probe timed out) — the honest stamp, never silently upgraded to a pass." Collapsing those two into a boolean is how ordinary test harnesses lie; keeping them apart is what lets _verdict distinguish "the effect is wrong" from "we cannot tell."

The ten probe kinds deserve a table, because the spec language is the product surface — a caller who can state their action's success in these terms gets verification; a caller who cannot has learned something important about their action:

file_absent{path}The filesystem: the path is gone — the deletion case, the PocketOS shape.
file_hash{path, sha256}The file's bytes: SHA-256 equals the declared hex — content, not just presence.
file_contains{path, text}The file's text contains the declared string.
file_changed{path}State differs from the pre-action snapshot (run mode only — observe mode has no "before").

Two catalogue decisions are worth copying. First, the probes span four different state surfaces — filesystem, HTTP, sockets, process table — because "effect" lives in different places for different actions, and a verifier confined to one surface would stamp most real actions UNVERIFIABLE. Second, timeouts are per-surface constants in the module (HTTP 5.0s, port 2.0s, command 30s): a probe that hangs is indistinguishable from a state that cannot be read, so the timeout is what converts an infinite wait into the honest third status.

GENESIS = "0" * 64


def _canonical(entry_without_hash):
    return json.dumps(entry_without_hash, sort_keys=True,
                      separators=(",", ":"))


def entry_hash(entry):
    payload = {k: v for k, v in entry.items() if k != "hash"}
    h = hashlib.sha256()
    h.update(entry["prev_hash"].encode("utf-8"))
    h.update(_canonical(payload).encode("utf-8"))
    return h.hexdigest()

1(g) Limitations, named plainly

  • The caller must declare postconditions. An action whose true success criterion nobody can state as a probe gets UNVERIFIABLE or, worse, a verified-but-vacuous spec. effectreckon verifies declared effects; it does not discover what the effect should have been.
  • Run mode executes shell actions only; other surfaces are covered by observe mode, which cannot verify file_changed (no pre-state).
  • The rollback hook is a shell command the caller supplies; effectreckon runs it on mismatch and records its exit, but does not verify the rollback's own effect unless the caller writes a spec for that too.
  • Probes read state at a moment in time; an effect that lands asynchronously after probing will read as a mismatch. Timeouts are per-action, not per-postcondition-settle.

Chapter 2 — foriscope: explain a system only as far as probes can prove

2(a) The business problem

Reverse-engineering tools had passed ~452K combined GitHub stars by November 2026. The category's output is an explanation: "this API works like this, this endpoint does that, this field means this." The scan found the category's explainers conceding, in their own documentation, that output "may be incomplete or incorrect" — a disclaimer that transfers all risk to the reader. The readers with the most at stake are doing technical due diligence: manual technical due diligence costs $20K–$200K per engagement, and 76% of technology acquisitions fail to meet their financial objectives. A wrong explanation of a target system's behavior is not an academic error; it prices a deal. And the explanations are produced by the same pattern as everywhere else in this document: a process (often a language model reading docs and code) asserts behavior, and nothing re-checks the assertion against the running system.

2(b) Options considered, and why Position 1 won

Option A — a better explainer (a stronger model, more context) improves prose quality but leaves the epistemics untouched: the output is still assertion. Option B — human review workflow is the $20K–$200K status quo. Position 1 — foriscope — changes what an explanation is allowed to be: a verified black-box explainer in which every behavioral claim must cite the probes that establish it, a claim counts as verified only when the same request issued twice returns agreeing responses, and any documented behavior that cannot be probed is not smoothed over — it is a reason-coded refusal, listed in the report next to the verified claims, with a coverage ledger closing the report so the reader can see exactly what fraction of the documented surface was actually established. Two further constraints define the tool: no language model is used at any point (the explainer cannot hallucinate fluently, because nothing in it generates prose from weights), and it refuses to run without an operator authorization attestation — probing someone else's system is an act that requires a named human's say-so, enforced by the CLI, not by a terms page.

2(c) The solution, step by step

  1. The operator supplies a base URL, an OpenAPI document (the discovery source), and the --authorized attestation. Without the attestation, the run stops.
  2. Discovery (discover.py) enumerates the surface from the OpenAPI document: endpoints, operations, documented behaviors.
  3. The prober (prober.py) issues requests. Every verification probe is replayed: the identical request is issued twice, and status plus body hash must agree. Every probe — purpose, method, URL, status — is written to the audit log as it happens.
  4. Claims (claims.py) are assembled only from probe evidence. A claim backed by agreeing replays is verified; a claim backed by a single observation, or by two disagreeing ones, is downgraded to single-observation — never silently upgraded; a documented behavior no probe can falsify is refused with a reason code from a fixed taxonomy.
  5. The report (report.py) renders every claim with its probe citations, every refusal with its reason, and closes with the coverage ledger: how much of the documented surface ended up verified, observed-once, or refused.
  6. The run's audit log is a hash-chained, HMAC-SHA256-signed clean-room record, independently checkable with verify-audit.

First, the replay contract, from the module docstring and the prober in foriscope/prober.py:

"""HTTP probing: every request goes through the audit log, twice.

A probe is one HTTP request made for one stated purpose. Verification
probes are *replayed*: the same request is issued twice and the two
responses must agree (status and body hash) before a claim built on them
can be called ``verified``. A claim built on a single observation, or on
two disagreeing observations, is downgraded - never silently upgraded.
"""
@dataclass
class ProbeResult:
    probe_id: str
    purpose: str
    method: str
    url: str
    status: int | None
    headers: dict = field(default_factory=dict)
    body: bytes = b""
    error: str = ""

    @property
    def body_hash(self) -> str:

Source: foriscope/prober.py.

Why replay? A single response proves the system did something once. Non-deterministic endpoints, caches warming, rate-limit fallbacks, and A/B behavior all make single observations unreliable witnesses. Requiring two agreeing observations (status and body hash) converts "we saw it" into "it reproducibly does this, at least across a probing session" — and, just as important, disagreement becomes information that downgrades the claim instead of noise that gets averaged away. Bodies are capped as evidence (MAX_BODY = 256 * 1024 bytes retained).

Second, the refusal taxonomy in foriscope/claims.py — the complete reason-code table and the deterministic classifier:

    "ORDER_SEMANTICS_NOT_VERIFIABLE": "an order is observable but the claimed criterion producing it is not",

    "WRITE_PROBES_NOT_ENABLED": "the operation changes state and write probing was not enabled for this run",
}
def classify_refusal(sentence: str, endpoint_status: int | None) -> str:
    """Choose the reason code for a candidate claim that cannot be probed.

    Deterministic keyword logic over the claim text plus the one hard fact
    the run does own: the status the endpoint returned without credentials.
    Authentication wins first, because it is a fact, not an inference.
    """

    if endpoint_status in (401, 403):
        return "AUTH_CONTEXT_UNAVAILABLE"

        return "INTERNAL_STATE_NOT_OBSERVABLE"
    if any(h in low for h in _TEMPORAL_HINTS):
        return "TEMPORAL_BEHAVIOR_NOT_PROBEABLE"
    if any(h in low for h in _UNIT_HINTS):
        return "UNIT_SEMANTICS_NOT_PROBEABLE"
    if any(h in low for h in _ORDER_HINTS):

Source: foriscope/claims.py.

Study the ordering. Authentication is checked first and from a fact (the endpoint actually returned 401/403 to an unauthenticated probe), not from the claim's wording. The remaining codes are keyword heuristics over the claim text — deterministic, inspectable, and honest about their nature. The taxonomy itself is the teaching content: UNIT_SEMANTICS_NOT_PROBEABLE ("probes see values, not meanings") names a class of claim — "this field is a price in USD" — that no black-box prober can ever verify, and that ordinary explainers assert constantly. ORDER_SEMANTICS_NOT_VERIFIABLE is subtler still: you can observe that a list is sorted; you cannot observe by what criterion it was sorted.

Decision — no model, on purpose Every other explainer in the scan uses a language model to write the explanation. foriscope's refusal to do so is the product decision: a claim set assembled by deterministic classification over probe records can be audited line by line; a fluent paragraph cannot. The cost — explanations that read like ledgers, because they are ledgers — is accepted deliberately.

One report element deserves its own paragraph, because it is the piece a due-diligence reader should turn to first: the coverage ledger that closes every report. It accounts for the entire documented surface — every behavior the OpenAPI document asserts — and sorts it into verified, single-observation, and refused, with the refusals carrying their reason codes. Its function is to prevent the oldest trick in audit reporting: emphasizing the checks that passed and letting the reader assume they were all the checks. A report that verifies 23 claims reads very differently when its ledger shows 27 documented behaviors accounted for than when the denominator is nowhere printed. The fixture result — 23 verified, 4 refused, 0 silently dropped — is a statement about accounting completeness as much as about probing. Any verification report you commission or consume, in any domain, should be required to close with the same three numbers: established, observed-but-not-established, refused — and the total they sum to. If the total does not reconcile with the surface the report claims to cover, the report has a hole exactly where its author chose not to look.

The authorization gate deserves the same emphasis from the other direction. foriscope will probe a live system — issuing real requests, twice each — and probing is an act performed on someone else's property. Requiring the operator's explicit --authorized attestation before the first request makes the legal and ethical precondition a runtime fact rather than a documentation hope, and the attestation is recorded in the signed audit log, so a report carries the name of the human who authorized its evidence-gathering. A verification tool that cannot show who authorized its probes has a gap in its own chain of custody — the same class of gap it exists to expose in others.

2(g) Limitations, named plainly

  • Black-box only: anything behind authentication without supplied credentials, and all server-side state, is refused by construction. A large fraction of real systems' interesting behavior lives exactly there.
  • Replay agreement is evidence within a probing session, not a guarantee: two agreeing responses minutes apart say nothing about behavior under load, at other times, or for other tenants.
  • The refusal classifier's non-auth branches are keyword heuristics. They are deterministic and inspectable, but a claim phrased unusually can land in the wrong refusal bucket — the stamp (refused) stays right; the reason may be imprecise.
  • Discovery is bounded by the OpenAPI document. Behavior the document doesn't mention is outside the coverage ledger entirely — the ledger measures coverage of the documented surface.

Chapter 3 — deedreckon: an independent verifier for agent-run companies

3(a) The business problem

The agent-company territory — frameworks that run a whole company of agents under one owner — is where the unverified-claim problem becomes a governance problem. Paperclip (115,510 stars) is the scan's seed, and the scan quotes its two most revealing design facts: it meters "reported" spend (the agents' own account of what they spent), and it tells the owner, in its own words, "You verify the output." The vendor of the automation declines to verify the automation. The surrounding evidence says owners should not take the reports at face value: CMU's TheAgentCompany benchmark puts the best agent's task completion at 30.3%; Gartner projects 40% of enterprises will demote or decommission autonomous agents by 2027 over governance gaps; Capgemini measured trust in fully autonomous agents falling from 43% to 27% in a year. And the Replit incident belongs to this chapter too: an agent deleted the production database during a code freeze and fabricated ~4,000 replacement records — a status report generated by the party whose work the report described.

The structural defect is independence. In financial life, the person who keeps the books is not the person who audits them. In agent-run companies, the same system writes the status report and grades it. What is missing is a checker that sits outside the operation, holds no stake in the narrative, and — critically — never acts: it cannot fix, send, deploy, or delete, so it cannot become another unverified actor.

3(b) Options considered, and why Position 1 won

Option A — richer dashboards inside the agent framework fails the independence test: the data path still runs through the system being reported on. Option B — a second "auditor agent" inside the company fails it differently: an LLM auditor's verdict is itself an unverified claim, and MAST's numbers (Chapter 5) show agent-checks-agent is where verification goes to die. Position 1 — deedreckon — is a non-agent verifier: claims arrive as plain files, each naming a checker and a system of record; deedreckon independently queries the record — GitHub, Gmail, a URL, a file, an HTTP JSON endpoint — and stamps the claim. It holds no write credentials, sends nothing, and its owner brief is generated only from verified state, so the brief cannot launder a refuted claim into prose. Deterministic checkers, not judgement, all the way down.

3(c) The solution, step by step

  1. Claims are written as plain data: an id, a statement, a checker name, a source (where the record lives) and an expect (what the record should show). Five checkers ship in v1: github, gmail, url, file, httpjson.
  2. deedreckon verify runs each claim's checker against the live system of record. The checker returns a verdict, a reason code, and the evidence it actually saw (URL, status, the record's fields).
  3. Every result is appended to the discrepancy ledger: append-only JSONL, hash-chained, recording claim, verdict, reason, and evidence. A re-check of the same claim is a new entry — history is never rewritten.
  4. deedreckon brief renders the owner brief from the ledger's latest verdicts, built only over verified state; refuted and unverifiable claims appear as discrepancies, not as facts.
  5. deedreckon chain verifies the ledger's hash chain on demand.

The semantics deserve a slow read. 404 → REFUTED / NOT_FOUND: the record was reached and the thing is not there — that is decidable contradiction. 401/403 → UNVERIFIABLE, with the body inspected to distinguish RATE_LIMITED from NO_ACCESS, because "GitHub wouldn't tell us" must never be misreported as "the claim is false." A malformed claim (no owner/repo) is UNVERIFIABLE / BAD_CLAIM — the tool blames the claim's form, not the claimant's honesty. This is the narrow-REFUTED discipline from §1.3 expressed as control flow. When the record is read, an expectation mismatch (a release tag that differs, a description that lacks the claimed text) is REFUTED / VALUE_MISMATCH, with the record's actual values attached as evidence.

Chapter 4 — podiumproof: was the tournament winner actually the best?

4(a) The business problem

Single-elimination tournaments judged by LLMs have become a standard selection mechanism: generate N candidate answers (or agents, or prompts), bracket them, let a judge model pick winners pairwise, crown a champion. The scan's seed is "Arena," a community skill by Jake Schincariol (1,564 stars, MIT) — the scan is careful to correct a circulating misattribution: Arena is a one-person community project, not an Anthropic product. The mechanism it popularized carries an implicit claim — the champion is the best candidate in the pool — and the arithmetic behind that claim is brutal. If the judge picks the genuinely better answer with probability 0.65 per match, the best answer must win 7 consecutive matches to take a 128-entry bracket: 0.657 ≈ 0.049. The best answer wins about 5% of the time. Nor is 0.65 pessimistic: JudgeBench measured prompted frontier judges at 50.9–64% accuracy on ground-truth pairs, and Panickssery et al. measured GPT-4's self-preference at 0.912 on CNN/DailyMail summarization — a judge that favors its own outputs at rates no bracket design can fix. Meanwhile the cost is real: a 100-agent bracket consumes ~595 sub-agent calls, justified, in the scan's phrase, against no baseline — nobody asks what a single greedy pass, or picking the median candidate, would have achieved for a fraction of the spend.

The business problem is that tournament outcomes are consumed as selections — this prompt ships, this agent is deployed, this answer is published — with the confidence of a measurement and the reliability of a coin weighted by judge bias. What is missing is an audit: after the tournament, using only the tournament's own recorded data, establish whether the crown means anything.

4(b) Options considered, and why Position 1 won

Option A — better judges (bigger models, ensembling, debiasing prompts) attacks the accuracy number but never produces evidence about this run: a generally-good judge can still have produced a noise champion on a pool with no headroom. Option B — re-run the tournament and compare champions doubles the cost to learn that two lotteries disagree. Position 1 — podiumproof — is a post-hoc selection audit that makes no model calls at all. It treats the finished tournament as evidence: the recorded matches fit a statistical model of this judge; the candidate pool is scored against an oracle where one exists (or a correct answer where supplied); the bracket is replayed 200 times with re-seeded draws under the fitted judge; and the run's cost is ledgered against honest baselines. The output is a stamp on the tournament's implicit claims, rolled into one of four verdicts: PREMIUM REAL, WITHIN NOISE, JUDGE ARTEFACT, or NO HEADROOM. Position 1 won because it is the only option that answers the question actually asked after a tournament — should I trust this champion? — and answers it from the run's own data at zero additional model cost.

4(c) The solution, step by step — the four checks

  1. Winner vs oracle (headroom and regret). Every candidate in the pool is scored against the oracle. Headroom is the gap between the best possible score and the field; regret is the gap between the oracle-best candidate and the crowned champion. If headroom ≈ 0, the tournament selected among equals (NO HEADROOM — the premium was never there to win). If regret is large, the bracket demonstrably passed over a better candidate.
  2. Champion stability (200 re-seeded replays). The bracket is re-simulated 200 times with fresh random draws, matches decided by the fitted judge model (below). The champion's reproduction rate — the fraction of replays in which the same candidate wins — measures whether the title is a property of the candidate or of the draw.
  3. Judge validity in situ. On the run's own recorded matches where the oracle separates the pair, judge accuracy is computed with a Wilson score interval. A two-parameter logistic fit yields alpha (position bias: how much being read first is worth, in log-odds) and beta (gap sensitivity: whether verdicts track true quality at all). Swap consistency is estimated from the fit: how often re-judging with presentation order swapped would crown the same winner. A planted-flaw canary — a candidate with a known defect — checks whether the judge catches what it must catch.
  4. Cost ledger. The run's actual call count is set against honest baselines (what the selection would have cost by simpler means), so the premium paid for the bracket is a number, not a feeling.

"""A judge model fitted to a run's own recorded matches.

For every recorded match where the oracle separates the two candidates,
we know the presentation order (the format lists the side the judge read
first as ``a``), the oracle gap, and who won. A two-parameter logistic
model describes that judge:

    P(first-listed wins) = sigmoid(alpha + beta * gap)

``alpha`` is the position bias: alpha > 0 means the side read first wins
more often than the quality gap justifies. ``beta`` is discrimination:
how strongly the judge's verdicts track the true quality gap at all.
Both are fitted by Newton-Raphson on the run's own matches with a light
L2 penalty, so the audit describes *this* judge in *this* run, not a
judge in general.
"""
def wilson(k, n, z=Z95):
    """Wilson score interval for a binomial proportion."""
    if n == 0:
        return (0.0, 1.0)
    p = k / float(n)
    denom = 1.0 + z * z / n
    centre = (p + z * z / (2.0 * n)) / denom
    half = (z * math.sqrt(p * (1.0 - p) / n + z * z / (4.0 * n * n))) / denom
    return (max(0.0, centre - half), min(1.0, centre + half))

Source: podiumproof/judgemodel.py.

The fitting is Newton–Raphson with a light L2 penalty and clamped steps "so separated data cannot run away" — small-sample honesty enforced numerically. The Wilson interval matters for the same reason: with, say, 30 decisive matches, an observed accuracy of 0.65 has a wide interval, and the audit reports the interval, not just the point. Note also the module's candor about swap consistency: a true swapped re-judgement would need the judge itself; offline, the model-based estimate is what exists, "and the report says so."

The constants are the audit's constitution, fixed before any fixture was run: a score gap of 0.02 or less is noise by definition; a champion who reproduces in fewer than half of re-seeded replays does not hold a stable title; swap consistency at or below 0.80 means presentation order decides matches. Because the thresholds are named constants in one file, a reader who disagrees with a verdict can point at the exact number they dispute — which is what makes the verdict an audit rather than an opinion.

The replay check (podiumproof/replay.py) is the piece most often misunderstood, so it is worth stating precisely what it does and does not do. It does not re-run the tournament: no candidate is regenerated and no judge is consulted — there is no judge process in the tool at all. It re-simulates the bracket: the same pool, fresh random draws for seeding and pairing across 200 replays, with each simulated match decided by sampling from the fitted judge model (the alpha/beta logistic of §4(e), fitted to the run's own recorded matches). The champion's reproduction rate is therefore a statement of the form: given a judge that behaves the way this run's judge measurably behaved, how often does this candidate win this bracket? That conditional is the honest one. A reproduction rate computed against an idealized fair judge would flatter every real run; computed against the run's own fitted judge — position bias, gap-blindness and all — it prices the title in the currency the tournament actually used. It also explains the fixture's most damning comparison: the flawed champion's 5.5% reproduction is below a random pick's expectation for a 16-candidate pool (1/16 ≈ 6.25%). The bracket did not merely fail to find the best candidate; as a selection device it underperformed drawing a name from a hat, at a cost of 91 calls. The cost ledger (check 4) exists to make that sentence computable for every audited run: calls spent, versus what the honest baselines — a single oracle-scored pass, a greedy selection, even random choice with the same evaluation budget — would have delivered. A tournament that cannot beat its baselines is not a selection mechanism; it is a ceremony with an API bill, and podiumproof's roll-up verdicts (PREMIUM REAL / WITHIN NOISE / JUDGE ARTEFACT / NO HEADROOM) are designed to say which of the four things a given run actually was.

4(g) Limitations, named plainly

  • The oracle leg needs an oracle. Where no ground-truth scoring or correct answer exists for the pool, the headroom/regret claims are UNVERIFIABLE and the audit leans on stability and judge validity alone.
  • Swap consistency is estimated from the fitted model, not measured by re-judging — podiumproof makes no model calls, so a true swapped re-judgement is outside its evidence. The report states this.

Chapter 5 — claimwitness: proof-carrying claims for agent channels

5(a) The business problem

Multi-agent systems run on messages: a builder agent tells a reviewer agent "tests pass," a researcher tells a writer "the file is updated," a worker tells an orchestrator "the command ran." The channel — a chat, a queue, a shared log — carries assertions, and the receiving agent's choices are to believe them or to redo the work. The MAST taxonomy (Cemri et al., NeurIPS 2025), from 1,642 traces across multi-agent frameworks, attributes roughly 21–24% of all multi-agent failures to task-verification failures: the system had a step whose entire job was checking, and the checking failed — typically because the "verifier" read the claim and nodded. The scan also records the cost extreme: a $47K, 11-day runaway Analyzer–Verifier loop, two agents confidently verifying each other in a circle, detected only at billing review. Adjacent findings sharpen the threat model: A2ASecBench documents attacks on agent-to-agent protocols, and framework-impersonation findings show agents accepting messages from parties that merely claim an identity. A channel claim therefore has two separate problems: who said it (authenticity) and is it true (verifiability). Ordinary messaging solves neither.

5(b) Options considered, and why Position 1 won

Option A — a verifier agent on the channel reproduces MAST's failure mode with extra steps: its verdicts are claims too. Option B — signed messages alone (authenticity without evidence) proves who lied, not what is true; a faithfully signed false claim is still false. Position 1 — claimwitness — makes claims proof-carrying: a claim is wrapped in an HMAC-SHA256-signed envelope whose evidence is machine-checkable against local ground truth, and verification means re-checking that ground truth — re-running the test command and comparing exit code and stdout hash, re-hashing the file — not re-reading the claim. The deepest design decision is a refusal: a bare "command-ran" claim about a past execution is stamped UNVERIFIABLE by construction, because a past execution leaves no local ground truth to re-check. The only way to make such a claim verifiable is to let claimwitness itself run the command at claim time, producing a signed run record. Position 1 won because it is the only option that distinguishes "unverifiable" from "false" at the protocol level instead of papering over the distinction.

5(c) The solution, step by step

  1. Each agent gets a key (keygen). Keys are per-agent secrets, stored like passwords.
  2. To claim, the agent builds an envelope: claim type, statement, and evidence — for tests-pass, the command and its observed exit code; for file-changed, the path and the file's SHA-256; for command-ran, optionally a signed run record produced by claimwitness running the command itself. The envelope is HMAC-SHA256-signed over every field.
  3. Any party holding the keyring can verify: the signature is checked first (a tampered envelope fails here), then the evidence is re-checked against ground truth, per claim type.
  4. Verdicts — VERIFIED / REFUTED / UNVERIFIABLE, each with the list of what was actually checked — are recorded in a hash-chained ledger.

The pattern to internalize is the three-way split in every verifier: missing evidence → UNVERIFIABLE (the claim was not checkable as made); evidence present but the world disagrees → REFUTED; evidence present and the world agrees → VERIFIED. The checked list is returned with every verdict, so a consumer can see what was actually done — "re-ran command in /repo; exit code 0" — rather than trusting the stamp alone. The command-ran verifier contains the design's honest core, in its own comment: "a past execution leaves no local ground truth. Without a signed run record there is nothing to check, so the claim is UNVERIFIABLE — never VERIFIED."

    cid, agent = env["id"], env["agent"]

        # ground truth. Without a signed run record there is nothing to
        # check, so the claim is UNVERIFIABLE -- never VERIFIED.

                       "no signed run record attached; a past execution "
                       "cannot be re-verified after the fact")

                       "run record's command does not match the claimed "
                       "command")

                       "not in the keyring, so its signature cannot be "
                       "checked")

Source: claimwitness/core.py, _verify_command_ran (opening section).

Trace the logic as a small lesson in evidence grading. No record at all → UNVERIFIABLE, with the reason stated as a principle, not an apology. A record whose command or exit code disagrees with the claim → REFUTED: here contradiction is decidable, because two signed artifacts disagree. A record whose signing key is not in the verifier's keyring → back to UNVERIFIABLE, for a subtler reason: the evidence exists but its authenticity cannot be established by this verifier, and authenticity-unestablished evidence must not be upgraded into a refutation or a verification. Only a record that matches the claim and carries a checkable signature from a known recorder can support VERIFIED. Three outcomes, three different evidentiary situations, and the code keeps them in separate branches — a claim system that collapsed the first and third branches ("no usable record") into the second ("mismatch") would be refuting claims on the strength of its own missing keys.

The protocol consequence is the teaching point: verifiability is decided at claim time, not at verify time. An agent that wants its "I ran the build" claims believed must route the execution through the recorder when it happens. Retrofitted verification is impossible for this claim type, and claimwitness is the rare system that encodes that impossibility in its type system instead of letting users discover it.

5(g) Limitations, named plainly

  • Ground truth is local. claimwitness verifies claims about this machine's files and commands. It cannot prove things about executions that happened elsewhere, in the past, without a run record — by design, and it says so in the verdict.
  • Re-running a test command re-executes arbitrary code from the evidence payload. Verification environments must treat claim evidence as untrusted input; the timeout bounds but does not sandbox execution.
  • Keys are shared secrets in a keyring file. Compromise of an agent's key lets an attacker sign false claims — which will still be REFUTED on re-check if their evidence is false, but the authenticity leg depends on key hygiene the tool cannot enforce.
  • Three claim types. New types require new verifiers; the envelope generalizes, the ground-truth checks do not write themselves.

Chapter 6 — memreckon: audit what the agent remembers

6(a) The business problem

Persistent memory is the fastest-growing layer in the coding-agent stack: stores that let an agent write down what it learned — file locations, conventions, test status, dependency versions — and read it back as trusted context in the next session. The memory scan scored 12 entrants across 6 dimensions. The verification column scored 0/12: not one entrant checks whether what is stored is true. The research says it often is not. HaluMem shows memories are fabricated at extraction (the store faithfully persists a claim the agent never had evidence for) and that update omission runs 37–98% (the world changes; the memory doesn't). Mem0's extraction F1 collapses to 6.22% at 1M-token scale. LongMemEval shows memory layers costing ~30% accuracy, with ChatGPT's memory dropping GPT-4o from 0.92 to 0.58 — the memory is worse than no memory. And the adversarial numbers are worse than the accidental ones: AgentPoison achieves ≥80% attack success at <0.1% poison rate, MINJA reaches 98.2% injection success with no write access, and a memory worm (TL-2026-6307) was disclosed on 2026-11-17, three days before this build. A memory store is a claim database that future sessions treat as ground truth. Nobody audits the claims.

6(b) Options considered, and why Position 1 won

Option A — build a better store (with verification as a feature) competes with 12 entrants and couples the audit to one write path; the moment a user picks a different store, the verification is gone. Option B — LLM fact-checking of memories reintroduces the unverified-claim problem inside the checker. Position 1 — memreckon — is not a store at all. It is a read-path verification layer that sits over any file-based store through adapters (memoryvault, markdown, auto), extracts claims deterministically, settles each against the live repo, and stamps it. The decisive novelty is a fourth stamp: STALE — the claim is still true, but the artifact it describes changed after the memory was written and the memory was never re-confirmed. STALE exists because memory rot is mostly not falsehood; it is truth that has quietly expired. A three-stamp system must call a stale memory VERIFIED (wrong signal) or REFUTED (wrong fact). Position 1 won because the layer, not the store, is where verification belongs — and because the audit must be read-only everywhere: an auditor that writes to the store it audits has destroyed its own evidence.

6(c) The solution, step by step

  1. An adapter reads the store (memoryvault format, plain markdown, or auto-detected) into entries, preserving each entry's recorded timestamp.
  2. Deterministic pattern extraction (no model) classifies entry text into claim types: file-exists, symbol-exists, test-outcome, dependency-version, config-value, command-outcome, git-state, and judgment (preferences, style opinions — mechanically uncheckable by construction).
  3. A per-type checker settles each claim against the live repo: does the file exist, is the symbol defined, does the manifest carry the claimed version, does the config hold the claimed value.
  4. The staleness rule applies to substantive passes: if the target artifact's mtime is newer than the memory's timestamp, the stamp is STALE, not VERIFIED. A fail is REFUTED outright — REFUTED always wins over STALE.
  5. Every verdict goes to the hash-chained ledger. Gate mode renders session-start context with REFUTED memories quarantined — present, but fenced, with their refuting evidence attached — so the next session sees the correction, not the rot.
  6. Safety rails: commands mentioned in memories are never executed unless --run-commands is explicitly passed; the tool is read-only everywhere.

The ordering is the semantics. Judgment claims short-circuit to UNVERIFIABLE — the tool refuses to pretend a preference is checkable. "Unable" (the checker could not decide, e.g. an ambiguous version spec) is UNVERIFIABLE, never a fail. "Fail" is REFUTED immediately, before staleness is even computed: a false memory is false, and calling it "stale" would understate it. Only a substantive pass reaches the staleness rule, which compares the newest target artifact's mtime against the entry's timestamp (with an epsilon) and downgrades to STALE with the full evidence — which artifact, modified when, memory written when. If the store recorded no timestamp, freshness is "not assessed" and the pass stands as VERIFIED with that caveat in the evidence.

6(g) Limitations, named plainly

  • Extraction is pattern-based. Memories phrased outside the patterns are classified as judgment or missed entirely — the checkable fraction of a real store is an empirical question the kill criterion exists to answer, and it has not been answered yet on three real stores.
  • STALE depends on timestamps: a store that records no entry timestamps makes freshness unassessable, and file mtimes are a proxy for "the artifact changed," not proof of semantic change.
  • File-based stores only, via the shipped adapters. A memory service behind an API is out of scope for v1.
  • memreckon detects false and stale memories; it does not repair them, and gate mode quarantines rather than deletes — the correction is surfaced to the session, the store is untouched (read-only is the safety story, and also the limit).

Chapter 7 — zombieaudit: recompute the track record before you believe it

7(a) The business problem

A prediction-market leaderboard is a track record claim at scale: this wallet made $X, wins Y% of the time, follow it accordingly. People allocate real money on these numbers — copying wallets, buying signals, sizing positions. The scan assembled the reasons to distrust the headline figures. Columbia University research estimates ~25% of Polymarket's historical volume is wash trading. Over 82% of ~313,000 wallets lost money, which makes the profitable tail — the part of the leaderboard anyone looks at — a selected, survivorship-shaped sample. PANews (January 2026) documented the zombie mechanism concretely: a top whale's 73.7% headline win rate falls to 53.3% once 1,791 zombie positions are counted — positions whose markets resolved against the holder, which sit unsettled at price 0 and value 0, never redeemed, and therefore never counted by a win rate computed over closed positions only. The headline rate is not fabricated; it is computed over a denominator that quietly excludes the losses. Worse, the venue's own surfaces disagree with each other: leaderboard and profile differ by ~$5M on the same wallet. And the venue's own agent framework is archived — the official tooling for this ecosystem is unmaintained. Nobody was publishing the adjustment.

7(b) Options considered, and why Position 1 won

Option A — a better leaderboard (recompute and republish rankings) inherits the venue's data problems and adds a new unverified claim source: ours. Option B — a wash-trading detector targets the loudest scandal but the hardest measurement; wash inference from public data is probabilistic, and a probabilistic accusation published as a verdict would violate the narrow-REFUTED discipline. Position 1 — zombieaudit — is a read-only attestation tool, not a leaderboard: for each top-50 wallet it re-aggregates the wallet's own public position records from the Polymarket Data API v2 — every lifecycle status, not just closed — deduplicates, cross-checks its sum against the venue's own per-wallet stats record, subtracts recorded fees, counts zombies, and stamps the headline claim VERIFIED / INFLATED / UNVERIFIABLE under pre-registered thresholds that are constants in the code. The wash-adjacency heuristic survives only as a flag, never as a verdict input — Option B's insight kept, its overreach refused. Position 1 won because attestation composes with any leaderboard (including the venue's own) and because its refusals — "the venue's two public aggregations of the same record do not reconcile" — are themselves the most valuable output.

7(c) The solution, step by step

  1. Fetch the top-50 leaderboard wallets, then walk each wallet's positions across every lifecycle status (CLOSED, REDEEMABLE, REDEEMABLE_LOST, MERGEABLE, OPEN) from the Data API v2 — GET requests only; the tool holds no keys and places no orders.
  2. Deduplicate by (condition_id, token_id): the API's status filters overlap, and the REDEEMABLE_LOST filter does not return the resolved losers it names, so rows are classified by their own fields, never by which query returned them.
  3. Recompute realized PnL by summing realized_pnl over settled positions. Cross-check against the venue's own /v2/user-stats record: a gap beyond 2% means the venue's two public aggregations disagree — a completeness failure that forces UNVERIFIABLE.
  4. Subtract the venue's recorded all-time fees to get fee-net PnL. (The leaderboard headline is the venue's pre-fee realized PnL; this single fact drives the first live run's headline result.)
  5. Count zombies — unsettled, current price 0, current value 0, redeemable: resolved losers never redeemed — and compute the win rate twice: headline-style (closed only) and zombie-adjusted.
  6. Apply the verdict rules (constants below) and write the report plus a JSON ledger. A snapshot mode re-runs the whole recomputation offline from a recorded snapshot, so any attestation is reproducible byte-for-byte from its evidence.

Chapter 8 — quantseal: a receipt for the exact file, not the label

8(a) The business problem

Local inference runs on quantized model files, and the ecosystem talks about them by label: "use the Q4_K_M." The label names a quantization recipe family. It does not name a file. Different publishers build Q4_K_M files from the same base model with different tooling versions, different calibration data, and different choices — producing different bytes with measurably different behavior — and the entire information ecosystem around them (leaderboards, calculators, forum advice) treats the label as the unit of record. The scan's seed is AirLLM (42,783 stars), whose headline "70B on 4GB" is VENDOR-CLAIMED; community measurements put its 70B decode at roughly 0.07–0.7 tok/s, and its quality cost is simply unpublished. The scan counted at least six calculator tools that answer "does it fit?" and none that answers "what does it cost in capability?" The joined layer — fit × speed × quality, per artifact — used to be approximated by the Open LLM Leaderboard, which retired in March 2025. Since then: nothing. A practitioner downloads a file on the strength of a label, runs it, and discovers its quality cost, if ever, anecdotally.

8(b) Options considered, and why Position 1 won

Option A — revive a leaderboard ranks models, not files, and a ranking is exactly the label-level thinking that causes the problem. Option B — publisher self-reporting asks the parties who benefit from flattering numbers to produce them; the AirLLM claim is what that produces. Position 1 — quantseal — fixes the unit of record: the file, identified by SHA-256. A receipt binds one exact file to one hardware profile and records size, decode and prompt speed, perplexity, KL divergence against the FP16 reference, and a frozen task-probe suite (12 reasoning, 10 instruction-following, 3 long-context retrieval items). Stamps are MEASURED / REFUTED / UNMEASURED. The query interface inverts the usual flow: quantseal check <file> hashes the file you actually have and answers from the ledger — and for any file not in the ledger, the answer is UNMEASURED, delivered before you run the model, when the information can still change your decision. Position 1 won because it is the only option whose claims are about artifacts a user can hold, checkable by re-hashing.

8(c) The solution, step by step — the measurement protocol

The lab protocol (recorded in measurement/PROTOCOL.md in the build tree) is frozen so receipts are comparable:

  1. Identify the file by SHA-256. The hash, not the filename or label, is the receipt's key.
  2. Measure size in bytes, exactly.
  3. Speed with llama.cpp's llama-bench: decode and prompt tokens/second on the receipt's hardware profile.
  4. Quality against the FP16 reference of the same base model: perplexity with llama-perplexity on a frozen 16,328-byte WikiText-2 slice, and --kl-divergence against saved reference logits — KLD measures how far the quantized file's output distribution has drifted from the reference, token by token.
  5. Run the frozen task-probe suite (12 reasoning + 10 instruction-following + 3 long-context retrieval items) under greedy decoding.
  6. Append the receipt to the ledger (ledger/receipts.jsonl — hashes and numbers, never weights; small enough to keep in git).
  7. Claim audits are receipts too: a vendor claim with no measurement behind it enters the ledger as UNMEASURED, with the community evidence on record noted as exactly that.

def sha256_file(path, chunk_size=1 << 20) -> str:
    digest = hashlib.sha256()
    with open(path, "rb") as fh:
        while True:
            chunk = fh.read(chunk_size)
            if not chunk:
                break
            digest.update(chunk)
    return digest.hexdigest()


def find_by_sha256(receipts, digest):
    """Return the measurement receipt for an exact file hash, or None."""
    for receipt in receipts:
        if receipt.get("kind") != "measurement":
            continue
        if (receipt.get("file") or {}).get("sha256") == digest:
            return receipt
    return None

Greedy decoding throughout removes sampling variance from the comparison: two receipts differ because the files differ, not because the dice landed differently.

Chapter 9 — edgereckon: audit the map the agents navigate by

9(a) The business problem

The seed was a short video about code knowledge graphs for AI coding agents — the "change one function, break 40 places" problem. The scan that followed verified 14 entrants live on 2026-11-21 and found the largest territory of the nine: Graphify at 151,552 stars, Understand-Anything 93,540, CodeGraph 78,313, codebase-memory-mcp 48,988 (MIT, C implementation, with a preprint), GitNexus 48,716, Serena 31,582 as the no-graph LSP control group, and Bloop archived and dead. These tools build a graph of the codebase — who calls whom, what depends on what — and sell the agent a map: query the graph instead of reading files; ask the graph what breaks if you change a function; trust the graph's edges.

The scan's findings, in order of severity:

  • Zero of 14 publishes call-graph precision or recall against ground truth. Not one entrant states, anywhere, what fraction of its claimed edges are real or what fraction of real edges it claims. The core quality numbers of the entire category are unpublished.
  • The published evidence says static graphs are badly wrong. Samhi et al. (ISSTA 2024) compared 13 static analysis tools against runtime ground truth and found they missed on average 61% of dynamically executed methods. An independent benchmark posted in codebase-memory-mcp's own issue tracker (#1382) measured graph recall at 0.525 — below plain grep at 0.723. The expensive map underperformed the text search it replaces, on the map's home metric.
  • Impact claims are uncalibrated. GitNexus presents impact analysis as rings — WILL BREAK / LIKELY AFFECTED — carrying confidence scores. Nobody has ever calibrated those scores against actual breakage. A confidence score with no calibration history is a label wearing a number's clothing (compare Chapter 8's quant labels: same failure, different territory).
  • Provenance is confused with verification. Graphify tags edges EXTRACTED / INFERRED / AMBIGUOUS. Those tags describe how the edge was produced, not whether it is true. An EXTRACTED edge can still be wrong (name resolution binds the wrong symbol, as §9(h) will show), and the tag gives the reader no way to tell.
  • Licensing traps hide in the leaders. GitNexus, at 48,716 stars, is PolyForm Noncommercial — source-available, not OSI open source. The category's second seed, codebase-memory-mcp (MIT), publishes a preprint (arXiv:2603.27277) claiming VENDOR-MEASURED 83% answer quality versus 92% for file exploration at 10× fewer tokens — honest numbers, and note what they concede: the graph answers worse than reading files, at a tenth of the tokens. Whether that trade is worth it depends entirely on which edges are wrong — the number nobody publishes.

The business problem, precisely: agents are making change decisions — what to edit, what to test, what is safe to skip — against call graphs whose error rates are unmeasured, and the cost of a wrong edge is asymmetric. A phantom edge wastes an afternoon of defensive testing. A missing edge ships a breakage. An impact answer presented without its evidence basis converts the graph's silent gaps into the agent's confident mistakes. What the territory lacks is not a better graph. It is an auditor for the graphs that already exist.

9(b) Options considered, and why Position 1 won

Option A — build a fifteenth graph tool, a more accurate one. Rejected on the scan's own evidence: accuracy claims without ground truth are the disease, and a new entrant's parser would face the same dynamic-dispatch wall that produces the 61% miss rate. The gap is not parsing quality; it is the absence of measurement. Option B — runtime-only tracing tools (skip static graphs; instrument everything) gives true edges for executed paths but cannot answer questions about the graph an agent is about to trust, and says nothing about code the tests don't reach. Option C — benchmark the vendors (a paper, a leaderboard of graph tools) produces a one-off study that ages immediately and helps nobody at the moment of decision. Position 1 — edgereckon — is the auditor: it takes a graph as a set of claims (from its own deliberately simple builder, or ingested from a Graphify-format export), obtains ground truth by running the repo's own test suite under tracing, stamps every claimed edge, computes the graph's precision and recall as measured quantities, checks whether the worktree has drifted since the graph was built, and answers impact queries only over the verified subgraph, refusing the remainder with reason codes. Position 1 won because it is composable with every entrant (any graph that can be exported can be audited), because its output is needed at decision time rather than publication time, and because it converts the category's rhetorical quality claims into numbers with denominators.

9(c) The solution concept: verified-or-refuse, applied to edges

Three concept decisions define edgereckon, and each is a refusal of a tempting shortcut:

Ground truth is execution. A caller→callee pair observed while the repo's own tests run is a fact: that call happened, in that run. Static analysis is not ground truth — it is another claimant. So the audit's second independent path (an AST census of what the worktree defines) is used only for what it can actually decide — existence — never as a substitute trace.

REFUTED is deliberately narrow — three reason codes only. An edge is REFUTED only when the evidence positively contradicts it: CALLEE_NOT_FOUND (the claimed callee is not defined anywhere in the worktree), CALLER_NOT_FOUND (same for the caller), or WRONG_SYMBOL (the caller's module defines a same-named local symbol, the trace shows the caller invoking that, and the claimed edge never fired). Everything else that fails to verify is UNVERIFIABLE with a reason that names the missing evidence: NOT_OBSERVED, CALLEE_NOT_EXECUTED, CALLER_NOT_EXECUTED, NO_GROUND_TRUTH, TARGET_OUTSIDE_REPO.

The asymmetry at the center Absence of observation is never refutation. A traced pair proves a call happened. The absence of a pair proves nothing — the tests may simply not exercise that path. Every stamp rule in edgereckon is built around this asymmetry; a tool that refuted unobserved edges would be punishing graphs for the test suite's coverage gaps, and its REFUTED counts would be as untrustworthy as the graphs it audits.

Impact answers inherit the stamps. An impact query runs over VERIFIED edges only, returns the verified blast radius by depth, and separately returns the refused remainder — every claimed incoming edge that could not be verified, with its stamp and reason — plus a drift flag if any file in the answer's neighborhood changed since the graph was built. The answer is complete only when the refused remainder is empty and nothing drifted. An incomplete answer says so, in the data, in a field a calling agent can branch on.

Source: edgereckon/graphbuild.py, module docstring.

The self-built graph is deliberately naive — name resolution by bare name, from-import, module attribute, or self. — because edgereckon is not competing with the graph builders. Its own graph exists so the audit can be demonstrated end-to-end and so the builder's blind spots (everything dynamic) become measurable recall misses instead of silent absences. The census is the subtler product: a second, independent extraction path whose only job is existence. When a claimed callee is absent from the census, the edge is refutable without any trace at all — the strongest cheap evidence in the system.

On every call event, the tracer resolves the frame to a symbol id (frames outside the repo root, and edgereckon's own frames, are excluded), records the callee as executed, and pairs it with the caller frame beneath it. audit.py — the six ordered rules, and the metrics

The stamping function is the chapter's most important code. Read the rule order as an argument about evidence strength:

def _stamp_edge(caller, callee, census, graph_nodes, trace, pairs,
                executed, callers_seen, traced_callees):
    # 1. Observed at runtime -- the strongest stamp, checked first so
    #    no refutation rule can override a call that demonstrably ran.
    if trace is not None and (caller, callee) in pairs:
        return VERIFIED, "OBSERVED", "pair executed under the traced test run"

    # 2. Existence refutation (trace-independent): the graph asserts a
    #    repo-internal symbol the worktree does not define.
    for role, sid in (("callee", callee), ("caller", caller)):
        if sid not in census and _repo_internal(sid, census, graph_nodes):
            node = graph_nodes.get(sid) or {}
            where = node.get("file") or model.split_symbol_id(sid)[0]
            return REFUTED, f"{role.upper()}_NOT_FOUND", \
                f"claimed {role} {sid} is not defined in the worktree " \
                f"(asserted location: {where})"

    # 3. Targets not asserted to be in this repo cannot be judged here.
    if callee not in census:
        return UNVERIFIABLE, "TARGET_OUTSIDE_REPO", \
            "claimed callee is not a symbol of this repo"

    # 4. No trace -> nothing more can be decided.
    if trace is None:
        return UNVERIFIABLE, "NO_GROUND_TRUTH", \
            "the repo's test suite produced no usable trace"
    # 5. Wrong-symbol refutation: the caller's module defines the same
    #    final name, the trace shows the caller invoking the local
    #    symbol, and the claimed edge never fired.
    caller_module, _ = model.split_symbol_id(caller)
    _, callee_qual = model.split_symbol_id(callee)
    callee_name = callee_qual.split(".")[-1]
    local_sid = model.symbol_id(caller_module, callee_name)
    if local_sid != callee and local_sid in census \
            and local_sid in traced_callees.get(caller, set()):
        return REFUTED, "WRONG_SYMBOL", \
            f"caller invokes the local symbol {local_sid} of the same " \
            f"name; the claimed target {callee} was never observed " \
            f"from this caller"

    # 6. Unobserved and not refutable: say which evidence is missing.
    if caller not in callers_seen:
        return UNVERIFIABLE, "CALLER_NOT_EXECUTED", \
            "caller never executed under the traced tests"
    if callee not in executed:
        return UNVERIFIABLE, "CALLEE_NOT_EXECUTED", \
            "callee never executed under the traced tests"
    observed = sorted(traced_callees.get(caller, set()))
    detail = "both endpoints executed; this pair was not observed"
    if observed:
        detail += f"; caller was observed calling: {', '.join(observed)}"
    return UNVERIFIABLE, "NOT_OBSERVED", detail

Source: edgereckon/audit.py, _stamp_edge.

Rule 1 first, deliberately: an observed call is un-refutable — no static argument may override a call that demonstrably ran (a monkey-patched or dynamically-dispatched edge that executed is VERIFIED even if the census-and-name logic would doubt it). Rule 2 needs no trace: non-existence is decidable from the census alone, but note the _repo_internal guard — the refutation only fires for symbols the graph asserts are in this repo; rule 3 then protects external targets (a stdlib or third-party callee is UNVERIFIABLE/TARGET_OUTSIDE_REPO, not a phantom). Rule 5 is the only trace-dependent refutation, and it is a positive contradiction: the trace shows the caller invoking a different, local symbol of the same name. Rule 6 is where the asymmetry lives: unobserved edges get a reason naming exactly which evidence is missing — caller never ran, callee never ran, or both ran but this pair wasn't seen (with the caller's observed callees listed, so a human can judge whether the suite plausibly covered the path).

  • precision_decidable = VERIFIED / (VERIFIED + REFUTED) — precision computed only over decidable edges. UNVERIFIABLE edges are excluded from the denominator rather than counted as errors, because the audit could not decide them; conflating "undecidable" with "wrong" would be the auditor committing the sin it exists to catch.
  • verified_share = VERIFIED / all claimed edges — the conservative companion number: what fraction of the graph you may actually rely on. The gap between precision_decidable and verified_share is the undecidable mass, made visible.
  • recall is measured against traced pairs whose endpoints are both defined in the census: of the calls that demonstrably happened (and that a graph of this repo could claim), what fraction did the graph claim? The report prints the traced-pair counts alongside, because a recall against a thin test suite is a lower bound with a visible denominator.

9(i) The hazards fixture, hazard by hazard

The fixture repo (fixtures/hazards_repo/, a small shop package with 7 tests) exists to make static-graph failure modes concrete. Each file isolates one mechanism that defeats naive call-graph extraction:

  • Registry / dict dispatch — shop/dispatch.py. notify() pulls its target from a dict: handler = HANDLERS[kind]; return handler(msg), with HANDLERS = {"email": send_email, "sms": send_sms}. A static graph sees no call edge out of notify at all; a naive graph might assert edges to every registry value. Only the email path is exercised by the tests, so a claimed notify → send_email is VERIFIED and a claimed notify → send_sms is UNVERIFIABLE/CALLEE_NOT_EXECUTED — the sms function exists, is registered, and is a real hazard for anyone who "cleans it up" as dead code on the graph's say-so.
  • DI container — shop/container.py. CheckoutService never names TaxService; the container wires them at runtime (register/resolve). The traced run proves the checkout → TaxService.tax edge is real. A static graph can see build_checkout instantiating classes but not the service-to-service edge — a guaranteed recall miss, of exactly the kind Samhi et al.'s 61% is made of.
  • Decorator registry — shop/events.py. Handlers register by decorator at import time (@on_event("ping")), and emit() dispatches through the registry dict: return _HANDLERS[name](payload). The emit → handle_ping edge exists only at runtime. The trace proves it; no static claim to it is possible from the call site.
  • String routing via globals() — shop/routes.py. ROUTES maps names to function names; route() resolves through globals()[ROUTES[name]]. There is no static call edge out of route() to claim. The tests exercise only home — so about_page is live code that neither the static graph nor the trace can say anything about, and the audit's census coverage numbers (31/36 executed) are where that ignorance is quantified.
  • Monkey-patching — shop/checkout.py + tests. express_total statically calls pricing.compute_tax — but in the test suite it is only ever run with shop.checkout.compute_tax patched out. The claimed edge therefore never fires under trace, while its caller and callee both execute in other contexts. The honest stamp is UNVERIFIABLE/NOT_OBSERVED — never VERIFIED (the call, as claimed, did not happen) and never REFUTED (in production, unpatched, it presumably does). This is the fixture's sharpest test of the asymmetry principle, and it is the case a cruder auditor would get wrong in both directions.
  • Name shadowing — shop/report.py. The module defines its own format_price; render() calls the bare name, which binds locally. A graph that resolves the name to shop.pricing:format_price asserts an edge the evidence positively contradicts: the trace shows render → shop.report:format_price. Stamp: REFUTED/WRONG_SYMBOL — one of only three refutation paths in the tool, exercised here by construction.
  • Planted phantom and stale edges — fixtures/graphify_hazards.json only. The hand-written Graphify-format graph additionally claims total_with_tax → shop.pricing:apply_discount (a function that does not exist anywhere — the INFERRED tag on it in the fixture is the vendor-provenance point made concrete) and receipt → shop.legacy:charge_card (a module that does not exist). Neither exists in the repo; both exist only in the graph file. These are the CALLEE_NOT_FOUND refutations, planted so the audit's precision number has known-correct inputs.

9(l) Limitations, named plainly

  • Ingestion breadth: v1 ingests Graphify-format node-link JSON plus its own builder. CodeGraph and GitNexus exports are not ingested — GitNexus additionally sits behind a noncommercial license that makes close integration a legal question, not just a technical one.
  • Ground truth is unittest-discovery only. A pytest-native suite that cannot run under stdlib discovery reports NO_GROUND_TRUTH and every edge stamps accordingly. This is honest and also the tool's biggest practical coverage limit: many real repos are pytest-native.
  • Drift is UNKNOWN for ingested graphs without source hashes. The Graphify fixture's drift verdict — UNKNOWN/NO_PROVENANCE — is the common case for foreign graphs today, because exporters don't record hashes. edgereckon reports the ignorance; it cannot retroactively create provenance.
  • The second static path is a census, not a second graph. Existence is independently checked; call resolution is not independently re-derived. A callee that exists but is never the true target (and whose local namesake the caller also never invokes under trace) will sit at UNVERIFIABLE/NOT_OBSERVED rather than REFUTED — the narrow-refutation discipline's cost, accepted knowingly.
  • Recall is bounded by the test suite. 31/36 census symbols executed is a good fixture suite; real suites are worse, and every unexecuted path is territory where the audit can stamp nothing stronger than UNVERIFIABLE. The metrics print their denominators so this bound is never hidden.
  • The real-repo leg is unassessed. The kill criteria were evaluated on the fixture only. Auditing three real open-source repos — the scan's harder leg, where the verified-fraction question stays open — has not yet run.

Chapter 10 — ontoreckon: check the graph against the meaning it declares

10(a) The business problem

The seed was a LinkedIn post by Mehdi Hamzeluie (2026-11-18, Beyond Knowledge Graphs: Why Ontology Matters in Multi-Hop AI Applications) arguing that connection is not meaning: a knowledge graph says that A relates to B; an ontology — classes and subclasses, properties, domain and range — says what A and B mean and how they may validly relate, so a multi-hop application can in principle judge whether a traversed chain is meaningful. The scan that followed verified 16 entrants live on 2026-11-21 and found the diagnosis right and the prescription unimplemented. The standards for everything the post describes (RDF, OWL 2, SHACL) are 8–25 years old and the validators are mature — and the LLM-era builders that produce nearly all new knowledge graphs consume none of it.

The scan's findings, in order of severity:

  • The dominant builders have no ontology to check against. Microsoft GraphRAG (36,717 stars) offers a configurable entity-type list while relation types are free text from extraction prompts; LightRAG (40,807 stars) lets the LLM choose entity and relation keywords; HippoRAG 2 and nano-graphrag are schema-free in the same way. The scan's extract-and-assert family — five entrants holding ~86,000 stars between them — produces the overwhelming majority of new agent-facing graphs, and in that family there is not a rejected ontology but an absent one.
  • Where enforcement exists, it is platform-bound and silent. TrustGraph (3,198 stars) is the strongest conformance story in the field: extracted triples are validated against domain and range, including the subclass hierarchy, and violators are rejected. But the rejection is silent — no per-triple stamp, no published violation rate for consumers — and it covers only graphs TrustGraph itself builds, inside its own runtime. The commercial semantic platforms (Stardog, GraphDB) enforce genuinely, at load and query time, inside platforms an enterprise team must adopt and operate. Enforcement is a property of the platform, never of the graph — and no platform audits graphs built by the other tools its customers also run.
  • The platform that takes an ontology as input disclaims the check in writing. Cognee (33,450 stars) accepts an RDF/OWL file as a reference vocabulary for entity grounding; its documentation states explicitly that strict mode does no domain/range, cardinality, or disjointness checking and that "no axioms are evaluated" — and edges are not conformance-checked at all. Graphiti (32,983 stars) types its extraction in Pydantic behind an edge-type map, but pairs with no mapping fall back to the raw extracted predicate, unschematized.
  • The classical substrate is mature, free, and disconnected. pySHACL will validate any RDF graph against SHACL shapes and return a conformance report — if a human authors the shapes (an OWL ontology does not automatically become SHACL shapes), converts the graph, and runs it by hand. Protégé with HermiT or Pellet will find an ontology's unsatisfiable classes — if an expert opens it. Nothing in this family knows what a GraphRAG export is, and nothing in the builder family knows this family exists.
  • Path validity is checked nowhere. Every entrant's multi-hop machinery — community search, Personalized PageRank, keyword traversal — is sophisticated about finding paths and silent about whether a path means anything. Paths are ranked by relevance, never refused on semantic grounds. A chain can be plausible hop by hop and invalid at the join, and it will still be answered.
  • The open-world trap is the conceptual root. In OWL, domain and range are inference axioms under open-world semantics: declaring that worksFor has domain Person does not make "Acme worksFor Alice" an error — it makes Acme a Person. Nothing is ever violated; things are only ever inferred. So "the graph has an ontology" is marketed as "the graph is validated" while, semantically, nothing was ever checkable. Even the benchmark that uses the phrase — Text2KGBench's "ontology conformance" metric — defines conformance in a way that explicitly skips domain and range.

The business problem, precisely: agents reason over knowledge graphs by traversing multi-hop chains whose triples were never checked against the meaning their builders declared — where any meaning was declared at all — and the moment of consumption, the traversal itself, is the one place no verdict exists. What the territory lacks is not another builder or another reasoner. It is an auditor that stands at the point of consumption: pipeline-agnostic, per-triple, per-path.

10(b) Options considered, and why Position 1 won

Option A — build a seventeenth builder, an ontology-first one. Rejected: extraction quality is not the gap; the gap is that no graph, from any builder, carries verdicts a consumer can act on. A new builder's output would be exactly as unchecked the moment it left its own pipeline — the TrustGraph border problem, reproduced. Position 2 — the conformance leaderboard (run the famous builders, publish their violation rates) produces a one-off study: valuable as evidence, but it ages with the next builder release and helps nobody at the moment an agent is about to traverse a chain. Position 3 — ontology pre-flight alone (a consistency screen for LLM-generated ontologies) checks the yardstick but never measures anything with it; the scan ranked it third, and ontoreckon folds it in as its first command rather than its whole product. Position 1 — ontoreckon — is the auditor: given a graph somebody else's tool built and the ontology that graph claims to follow, it stamps every triple CONFORMANT / VIOLATING / UNVERIFIABLE with reason codes, publishes the violation rate over the triples the evidence could decide, checks the ontology itself before trusting it as a yardstick, and audits multi-hop answer paths — answering a path only when every hop exists, every hop conforms, and every join composes. Position 1 won because it is composable with every entrant (any graph that can be exported to triples can be audited), because its verdicts are needed at consumption time rather than publication time, and because it prices the open-world trap instead of falling into it: the semantic reading behind every stamp is declared in the report header.

10(c) The solution concept: verified-or-refuse, applied to triples and paths

Three concept decisions define ontoreckon, and each is a refusal of a tempting shortcut:

The closed-world reading, declared openly. ontoreckon deliberately re-interprets the profile's declared domain, range, cardinality and disjointness as constraints, under a closed-world reading, and prints that reading in the header of every report. This is the reading under which a declared ontology can stamp verdicts at all, and it matches what builders and buyers uniformly believe their declarations mean. Its known cost — a declaration written for OWL inference may stamp VIOLATING where a reasoner would infer — is named in the README and accepted by the user who points the tool at a profile. The fixture's known-good arm exists to keep the reading honest: it must produce zero false VIOLATING stamps, and validate.py enforces that at 0%, stricter than the scan's 5% kill-criterion bar.

The design decision at the center In OWL, nothing is ever violated — things are only inferred. Stating that worksFor has domain Person makes "Acme worksFor Alice" evidence that Acme is a Person, not an error. A conformance verdict is therefore impossible until someone declares which reading is in force. ontoreckon declares it — closed-world, constraints — in the header of every report it emits, so a stamp can always be traced back to the semantics it was issued under. A conformance claim that does not state its reading is the open-world trap wearing a checkmark.

VIOLATING is narrow, and missing evidence is never conformant-by-default. A triple stamps VIOLATING only on positive contradiction of a declared constraint, under five reason codes: DOMAIN_VIOLATION, RANGE_VIOLATION, DISJOINT_CLASS, CARDINALITY_BREACH, UNDECLARED_PROPERTY. An untyped entity or an undeclared class makes the triple UNVERIFIABLE (UNKNOWN_CLASS, UNTYPED_ENTITY) — the graph's poverty of evidence is reported as the graph's poverty, not laundered into either innocence or guilt. The violation rate is likewise computed only over decidable triples, with the decidable base printed next to it.

A path is answered only when it is valid on three independent grounds. Existence (every hop triple is in the graph), composability (at every join, the range of the incoming relation and the domain of the next share a class under the subclass hierarchy — otherwise no typing of the join entity could make both hops conformant), and hop verdicts (every hop stamps CONFORMANT). A failing path is PATH_REFUSED with the failing hop or join and its reason code named. Composability is schema-level — a property of the path pattern, decidable from the profile alone — and where a join's constraints are undeclared, the join is JOIN_UNCONSTRAINED, which also refuses: ontoreckon answers only verified paths.

  1. load: the ontology is parsed from either format into the single internal profile; out-of-subset declarations are recorded as PARSE_UNSUPPORTED with line numbers, never silently dropped, and play no part in any verdict.
  2. pre-flight: the profile is screened for unsatisfiable classes, disjoint-self declarations, subclass cycles and dangling references. audit runs pre-flight first and embeds its verdict in the report — stamps against a broken ontology are produced, but the report header says the yardstick failed, so they are never presented as trustworthy.
  3. stamp: every triple passes through the precedence-ordered checks in audit.py (§10(f)): undeclared property, missing typing, unknown class, domain, range, cardinality. Typing triples are checked for disjoint conflicts as a property of the entity's typing as a whole, and cardinality is computed in a pre-pass over (subject, predicate) groups.
  4. summarise: stamp counts, the violation rate over decidable triples, and per-code and per-relation breakdowns — so a consumer can see which of the builder's relations carry the violations.
  5. path (separate command): an explicit path, or every walk from a start entity along a relation sequence, is audited for existence, composability at each join, and per-hop stamps. path exits 0 only when the path is PATH_VALID; preflight exits 0 only when the ontology is CONSISTENT — the verdicts are in the exit codes, so both compose into pipeline gates without parsing JSON.

10(f) Code walkthrough, module by module

model.py — the whole "reasoner" is set closure

Under the declared closed-world reading, every check in the tool reduces to membership tests against the subclass closure of a finite profile — no OWL reasoner is involved, and none is needed. model.py precomputes exactly those closures:

    def is_subclass(self, cls: str, of: str) -> bool:
        """True iff ``cls`` is ``of`` or a (transitive) subclass of it."""
        return of in self.self_and_ancestors(cls)

    def disjoint_pairs_among(self, classes: set[str]) -> list[tuple[str, str]]:
        """Declared disjoint pairs (A, B) such that some member of
        ``classes`` is a subclass-or-self of A and some (possibly the
        same or another) member is a subclass-or-self of B."""
        hits = []
        for a, b in self.disjoint:
            under_a = any(self.is_subclass(c, a) for c in classes)
            under_b = any(self.is_subclass(c, b) for c in classes)
            if under_a and under_b:
                hits.append((a, b))
        return hits

Source: ontoreckon/model.py, Ontology.

Two documented simplifications live here, both stated rather than hidden: entities, classes and properties are identified by local name (the text after the last :, # or / in an IRI), so two IRIs with the same local name are the same symbol in the subsets ontoreckon parses — a documented simplification of the subsets, not a claim about RDF; and the subclass walk is cycle-safe — a subclass cycle yields the reachable set, and the cycle itself becomes a pre-flight finding rather than a hang. The profile is small on purpose: classes with parents, properties with optional domain / range / max_cardinality, disjoint pairs. Everything else OWL can say is out of the subset, and out-of-subset input is refused by name (PARSE_UNSUPPORTED), not approximated.

pathaudit.py — the join is where paths die

def composability(onto: Ontology, pred_in: str, pred_out: str) -> tuple[str, str]:
    """(status, detail) for the join pred_in -> pred_out.

    status: COMPOSABLE / NON_COMPOSABLE / UNCONSTRAINED.
    """
    p_in = onto.properties.get(pred_in)
    p_out = onto.properties.get(pred_out)
    if p_in is None or p_out is None:
        return "UNCONSTRAINED", "an undeclared property takes part in the join"
    if p_in.range is None or p_out.domain is None:
        return "UNCONSTRAINED", (
            f"range({pred_in})={p_in.range} or domain({pred_out})={p_out.domain} undeclared")
    common = onto.subclasses(p_in.range) & onto.subclasses(p_out.domain)
    if common:
        return "COMPOSABLE", f"common class(es) under range/domain: {sorted(common)}"
    return "NON_COMPOSABLE", (
        f"no class is a subclass of both range({pred_in})={p_in.range} "
        f"and domain({pred_out})={p_out.domain}")

Source: ontoreckon/pathaudit.py, composability.

The composability test is pure set intersection over subclass closures: the join composes exactly when some class could type the join entity so that both hops conform. No semantics are invented — if the declared range and the next declared domain admit no common class, the refusal is a theorem about the profile, not an opinion about the world. audit_path then checks the three grounds in order (existence, composability, hop verdicts) and refuses with the first failure named; enumerate_paths applies the same audit to every walk from a start entity along a relation sequence, so an agent can ask where a relation chain actually leads that it may trust, and get only PATH_VALID walks answered.

graphload.py — a small loader with a loud failure mode

The graph loader parses the N-Triples subset — one <s> <p> <o> . triple per line, string literals as objects, type as the membership predicate — sharing its line grammar with the ontology loader. Its design decision is the failure mode: any non-blank, non-comment line outside the subset grammar is returned as PARSE_UNSUPPORTED with its line number, listed in the report, and takes no part in any verdict. A loader that silently skipped lines it could not parse would be manufacturing conformance out of its own blindness; the count of unparseable lines is itself audit evidence about the export.

  • Disjoint-class membership — Pat. Pat is typed both Person and Organization, which the profile declares disjoint. Because the conflict is a property of the entity's typing as a whole, both typing triples stamp VIOLATING / DISJOINT_CLASS — the fixture's 2 disjoint stamps.
  • Cardinality breach — Carol's two managers. hasManager declares max_cardinality 1; Carol has two distinct managers. The excess triple (objects ordered by name, deterministically, so the choice of which triple breaches is reproducible) stamps VIOLATING / CARDINALITY_BREACH.

  • Range violation — Bob worksFor Jacksonville. The range of worksFor is Organization; Jacksonville is a City. VIOLATING / RANGE_VIOLATION.
  • Undeclared property — Alice likes Bob. likes is not in the profile at all. VIOLATING / UNDECLARED_PROPERTY — checked first in the precedence order, because a relation the ontology never declared is a positive contradiction available before any typing evidence is consulted.
  • Missing evidence — Ghost, and Zed the Wizard. Ghost appears with no type triple (UNVERIFIABLE / UNTYPED_ENTITY); Zed is typed with Wizard, a class the profile does not declare (UNVERIFIABLE / UNKNOWN_CLASS). Together with the further untyped/unknown cases they make the fixture's 5 UNVERIFIABLE — the stamps a type-poor real export would accumulate at scale.

The path audit must refuse it: PATH_REFUSED / NON_COMPOSABLE_JOIN at hop 2. This is the scan's H2 gap in miniature: a chain that is plausible hop by hop and semantically invalid at the join.

10(l) Limitations, named plainly

  • The verdicts are only as good as the profile. ontoreckon checks a graph against the ontology it is given. A wrong or thin ontology yields stamps that are mechanically correct and substantively meaningless; pre-flight screens the profile's internal consistency, not its fidelity to the world.
  • Closed-world is a stance, not a fact. Profiles authored for OWL inference may stamp violations an OWL user would call inferences. The reading is declared in every report header; accepting it is the user's decision when they point the tool at a profile.
  • Ingestion breadth: v1 graph input is the N-Triples subset only. GraphRAG / LightRAG JSON entity-relation exports are not ingested — a converter is future work, named in the coverage statement rather than silently absent.
  • Composition rules are the derivable ones only. v1 implements the composability rule derivable from the profile itself (domain/range chaining under subclass closure). Ontology-declared property-chain and composition axioms are not in the v1 profile format and are not checked — a path a chain axiom would legitimate can be refused.
  • Cardinality is maximum-only, unqualified. OWL min-cardinality and qualified cardinality are not expressible in the subset.
  • Composability is schema-level. A join is composable when the declared range and the next declared domain share a subclass — a property of the path pattern. ontoreckon does not invent composition semantics the ontology does not declare, and instance-level plausibility beyond the hop stamps is out of scope.
  • Most real exports are type-poor. If most triples come back UNVERIFIABLE, that is the audit result — the graph cannot be checked as shipped. The publication-stage kill evaluations have not run: criterion (i), the share of UNVERIFIABLE across three real GraphRAG/LightRAG exports, and criterion (iii), the competitor-audit re-check, are evaluations for the publication stage, not build-stage checks; only the fixture arm of criterion (ii) is enforced at build.

Chapter 11 — embedreckon: stamp the index before the migration ships

11(a) The business problem

The seed was a short video by Glue Info (2026-11-20) posing a forward-deployed-engineer interview scenario. A team ships a new embedding model. Overnight, AI search over 20 million documents breaks completely — not with an error, but with wrong answers: live queries are embedded with the new model while the 20M stored documents still carry old-model vectors. The two vector spaces are incompatible, so similarity search silently returns plausible-looking, semantically wrong documents. Accuracy collapses; nothing logs anything. Re-embedding everything against the live database would take search down for roughly three days. The interview question: fix 20M vectors without stopping search. The scan that followed verified 24 entrants live on 2026-11-22 and found that the scenario is not a contrivance — it is the default failure mode of the entire stack, and the migration mechanics the interview fishes for are the solved part. What is missing is everything underneath them.

The scan's findings, in order of severity:

  • Vector stores enforce shape, not space. A store checks dimension and distance metric, and nothing else — and shape is exactly the property that fails to protect you. Embedding spaces from different models are incomparable even at identical dimensions: OpenAI's text-embedding-3-small can emit 1,536 dimensions, exactly ada-002's width, and the vectors still live in different geometry; Google's own documentation states its gemini-embedding-001 and gemini-embedding-2 spaces are incompatible and all data must be re-embedded. A well-formed float array of the right dimension has nothing to be rejected for: the query succeeds, the results are ranked, the ranking is meaningless.
  • Provenance, where it exists, binds configuration — never individual vectors. The strongest record in the field is OpenSearch's semantic field, which writes model ID, name and type into generated field metadata — for vectors the platform embeds itself, under a cluster-internal opaque identifier; any externally computed vector bypasses it entirely, and the raw-write path records nothing. Weaviate, Vespa, Elasticsearch and Pinecone's integrated inference likewise record which model was configured, per collection or per field. Milvus and pgvector have no model fields at all. No entrant binds a stored vector to the model and version that produced it in a way a later audit can check.
  • Migration mechanics are good; migration verification is empty. Qdrant publishes an official zero-downtime migration tutorial — blue-green collections, alias swap, named vectors for rollback — and 2026 runbooks repeat the dual-write / shadow / backfill discipline. Its verification step, in the tutorial's own framing, is that the operator should "compare retrieval quality on your own queries before you switch." Which queries, how many, what overlap threshold, what proof that all 20M documents made it across, what happens if the backfill silently dropped 2% — left as an exercise. No entrant offers a completeness proof, a single-space proof, or a certificate of any kind.
  • Retrieval evaluation cannot attribute this failure. RAGAS, TruLens, DeepEval and Promptfoo evaluate the outputs of a live pipeline — answer quality, context precision, faithfulness. None accepts an index as input. A mixed-space index degrades their metrics exactly the way bad chunking, a weak model or stale content does, so a team can tune prompts for weeks against a symptom whose root cause no tool in the family can name. MTEB, meanwhile, benchmarks models in isolation: the industry's informal compatibility evidence is a leaderboard delta, a statement about a benchmark corpus, not about your index.
  • Compatibility evidence is documentation, not measurement. The field's clearest compatibility statement — Voyage's documentation that all voyage-4-series models share one vector space — is a sentence. The research literature reframes the question without productizing an answer: Vec2Vec (NeurIPS 2025) proves spaces can be translated into one another without paired data, with an explicit security warning from its own authors that unattributed vector databases can be mined for document attributes; relative representations enable zero-shot stitching measured on benchmarks; TellTail proves a deployed retriever's identity leaks through its behavior — perfect identification from full rankings across 53 retrievers — as an attacker's reconnaissance tool, not as an audit a system owner can run on stored vectors.
  • The forced-migration clock is ticking. OpenAI shut down 16 first-generation embedding models on 2024-01-04 (and covered users' re-embedding costs — an admission of where the cost lands); Cohere's embed v2 family is scheduled for shutdown on 2026-04-04; Azure's catalog lists retirement dates in February 2028 for the text-embedding-3 family and ada-002. Every production index is one provider deprecation notice — or one careless deploy that flips the query-side model first — away from the reel's scenario.

The business problem, precisely: a vector index is a claim — "these vectors all live in the space this manifest declares" — that nothing stamps, and a migration is a claim — "the new index is complete, single-space, and retrieves equivalently" — that nothing certifies before cutover, when the cost of being wrong is the reel's silent collapse. What the territory lacks is not a better store or a better runbook. It is an auditor for the indexes that already exist, and a certifier that can refuse a migration before the traffic moves.

11(b) Options considered, and why Position 1 won

Option A — a store plugin or a vendor feature. Rejected on the scan's own evidence: provenance that lives inside one platform is the OpenSearch position — real, and bounded by that platform's walls and its raw-write bypass. The failure mode is cross-platform by construction (the vectors were written by some pipeline, into some store, over years). Position 2 — the planted-mix measurement report (build a silently mixed index, ask what the field's tools say about it, publish) produces a one-off study: strong evidence, immediately aging, and of no use to the team standing at a cutover decision tonight. Position 3 — the ingest-side manifest writer (an embedding bill of materials written at ingest time) is prevention at write time — genuinely valuable, and the scan ranked it third precisely because it does nothing for the indexes that already exist, which is all of them. embedreckon consumes manifests in v1; the writer is a separate tool, not built. Position 1 — embedreckon — is the auditor and certifier: given a vector export and a declared provenance manifest, it audits every partition; given an unprovenanced population, it screens it for mixed-space contamination under a published refusal boundary; given paired embeddings of the same texts from two models, it measures whether their spaces can be aligned and with what loss; and given an old index, a new index and a golden query set, it certifies or refuses a migration before cutover. Position 1 won because it works on exports any store can produce, because its verdicts are needed at the two moments the territory currently runs on trust — "what is actually in this index?" and "may we cut over?" — and because its differentiation was re-checked at build time (the scan's kill criterion (iii)): no vendor ships a per-partition mixed-space audit or a migration certification. The nearest adjacent entrant is an ingest-side signing/policy gateway — write-time prevention, waitlist-stage — a different layer.

11(c) The solution concept: verified-or-refuse, applied to indexes

Four concept decisions define embedreckon, and each is a refusal of a tempting shortcut:

The refusal boundary is printed in every report. embedreckon stamps populations and partitions, never individual vectors; it never attributes a vector to a named model. A statistical null result is SINGLE-SPACE-CONSISTENT, never "verified single-space": contamination whose negative-tail signal falls in the registered refusal band stamps UNVERIFIABLE — not a guessed MIXED, and not a false CONSISTENT. Statistics alone never certify a migration: certification requires the provenance audit, the completeness check and measured retrieval equivalence to all pass, and any leg that cannot be evaluated refuses the certificate. The boundary text ships in the refusal_boundary header of every report the tool emits, so a consumer cannot receive a verdict without receiving the terms it was issued under.

The dual-use boundary, as design The tool ships no translator and makes no per-vector claims — on purpose. The research line (Vec2Vec and its successors) demonstrates that embedding spaces can be translated into one another without paired data, and that unattributed vector databases leak document attributes under that translation. A verification tool that shipped a translator, or that named which model produced an individual vector, would be building the attack it audits against. So the compatibility probe measures a pair the user declares, on pairs the user supplies, and its alignment map is computed inside the measurement and discarded — it is not an exportable artifact. The boundary is recorded in COMPLIANCE.md as an original design decision.

MIXED is reserved for positive evidence — and disagreement without a majority is refused, not guessed. The audit's cross-partition leg compares partitions claiming the same model pairwise; the largest mutually agreeing set defines the claimed geometry, and a partition deviating from that set beyond the mixture floor is MIXED. But two partitions that disagree with no majority between them are both UNVERIFIABLE (SIGNATURE_INCONCLUSIVE): the audit will not guess which one is foreign. A 50/50 disagreement is a fact about the evidence, and the stamp says so.

The probe's strongest word is "aligned, with measured loss." The verdict COMPATIBLE, unqualified, does not exist in the tool — a test asserts it never appears in any output. Every alignment the probe can measure loses something (a neighbourhood preservation below 1.0, a Procrustes residual above 0), and the loss is the deliverable: ALIGNED-WITH-MEASURED-LOSS carries the numbers; INCOMPATIBLE is stamped only when raw statistics are at chance and no orthogonal map recovers neighbourhood structure; everything between is UNVERIFIABLE, with the same-family refusal (family-level signatures do not support version-level claims) named where it applies.

Certification has three legs and no partial credit. Completeness (the live id set exactly — missing ids and resurrected tombstones refuse), single-space (the new index passes the provenance audit, and every partition is claimed by the manifest), and retrieval equivalence (golden-set top-k overlap against a declared threshold). Any leg FAIL — or UNVERIFIABLE — refuses the certificate, with every leg's measured values in the report either way. There is no partial certification, because a certificate that passes two legs of three is precisely the artifact that would have waved the reel's migration through.

11(e) The run, step by step

# 2. Statistical screen: no provenance, population level only
embedreckon screen --export population.jsonl --out report.json

# 4. Migration certification: refuse or certify before cutover

    --manifest new-manifest.json --golden golden.jsonl \
    --threshold 0.35 --out certificate.json

Source: embedreckon README, commands.

  1. audit: each partition of the export is resolved against the manifest's claim, then passes five legs — claim/record-provenance agreement, dimension, duplicate ids, truncation signature, and the within-population screen plus cross-partition signature analysis (§11(f)). The stamp is MIXED if any positive-evidence reason fired, UNVERIFIABLE if only missing-evidence reasons fired, CONSISTENT only if no reason fired — with the legs that ran listed in the report.
  2. screen: each partition (and, in the CLI's single-population form, the export as one population) is first checked for the signatures the statistic cannot honestly read — too small, inconsistent dimensions, uniformly truncated, quantized — and refused on those grounds before any mixture statistic is computed. Only then is the negative-tail fraction measured against the registered ceiling and floor.
  3. probe: raw same-text cosine is measured against a shuffled-pairing baseline; unmapped neighbourhood preservation is computed; if raw geometry does not already align, an orthogonal Procrustes fit is measured three ways — residual, mapped preservation, and a hold-out preservation with the rotation fit on half the pairs and evaluated on the other half, so the fit cannot grade its own homework.
  4. certify: the three legs run independently and are all reported with their measured values; the verdict is CERTIFIED only when all three PASS. The single-space leg re-runs the audit on the new index and additionally refuses any partition the manifest does not claim — certification binds to the manifest's declaration, not to self-declared record provenance.

11(f) Code walkthrough, module by module

stats.py — one statistic, with its thresholds registered before the data

The screen's entire evidentiary basis is one observation about geometry, stated in the module docstring: in a single embedding space whose population shares any background component (real corpora always induce one — common topics, common vocabulary, a shared mean direction), strongly negative pairwise cosines are rare; vectors from a different space are oriented independently of the population, so cross-space pairs produce strongly negative cosines at a substantial rate. The statistic is the fraction of sampled pairs whose cosine falls below a threshold — and the verdict thresholds are named constants, fixed at build time from the fixture arms, not tuned per input:

TAU_NEG = -0.05          # "strongly negative" cosine threshold
CLEAN_CEILING = 0.0015   # at/below: consistent with a single space
MIXTURE_FLOOR = 0.010    # at/above: mixture signal
SCREEN_PAIRS = 6000      # sampled pairs per statistic (default)
MIN_POPULATION = 300     # below this, the screen refuses
MIN_REFERENCE = 300      # cross-partition reference minimum
MIN_PARTITION_STAT = 100  # cross-partition target minimum

Source: embedreckon/stats.py.

Between CLEAN_CEILING and MIXTURE_FLOOR lies the refusal band — the range where the measured separation does not support either stamp — and everything in it is UNVERIFIABLE. This is §3.6's Move 3 (pre-register thresholds as named constants) applied to the tool's own detector: the band is why a 1% foreign tranche is refused rather than called, in §11(j)'s measured arm. The same module carries the alignment machinery: procrustes computes the orthogonal fit via a stdlib Jacobi eigendecomposition (no numpy — there is no numpy), returning the rotation, the relative residual, and the smallest/largest singular-value ratio, which reports how well-conditioned the alignment is: near zero, the fit is fitting noise.

screen.py — refusal first, statistic second

Read the order of screen_population as the boundary enforced in code. Before any mixture statistic is computed, the population is checked for the three conditions under which the statistic cannot honestly be read, and each is a refusal, not a caveat:

A uniformly truncated population is a legitimate reduced-dimension space — stamping it SUSPECT-MIXED for failing a full-dimension null would be the detector manufacturing its own positives, so the screen refuses (TRUNCATED_POPULATION). Quantization moves exactly the statistics the screen reads, so sensitivity cannot be stated honestly over quantized vectors (QUANTIZED_POPULATION). Only a population that survives these guards earns a statistic, and the statistic then stamps SUSPECT-MIXED at or above the floor, SINGLE-SPACE-CONSISTENT at or below the ceiling — note the verdict's wording: consistent, never verified — and UNVERIFIABLE / BELOW_SEPARATION_FLOOR between them.

audit.py — five legs, and the leg that catches the quiet case

The audit's legs run from bookkeeping to geometry: claim resolution and record-provenance agreement (a partition with no manifest claim and no per-record provenance is UNSTAMPED; records whose stamped model or version disagrees with the claim are positive evidence — MANIFEST_MISMATCH), dimension, duplicate ids anywhere in the export, and the truncation signature (a tranche of vectors whose trailing dimensions are exactly zero — truncated, then repadded to restore the declared dimension). The fifth leg is the one the tool exists for:

Partitions claiming the same model are compared pairwise with the cross-partition negative-tail statistic; the largest set of mutually agreeing partitions defines the claimed geometry; a partition outside that set whose statistic against the set's pooled vectors reaches the mixture floor is MIXED / CROSS_PARTITION_SIGNATURE_MISMATCH. This is the leg that catches a partition written entirely by the wrong model while every record claims the declared one — a partition whose own population is a perfectly consistent single space, the wrong one, so no within-partition check can see it. And the branch shown above is the discipline's other half: with no majority agreement set, deviation is recorded as SIGNATURE_INCONCLUSIVE and the partition stamps UNVERIFIABLE. The audit reports the disagreement; it does not break the tie by fiat.

The probe consumes paired embeddings the user supplies — the same texts embedded by two models — and answers the scan's H4 question with measurements instead of documentation: raw same-text cosine against a shuffled baseline (are the spaces already the same geometry?), unmapped neighbourhood preservation (do the spaces induce the same top-k neighbour sets?), then the Procrustes route with its residual and mapped preservation. The hold-out construction is the honesty mechanism: a rotation fit on all pairs and evaluated on the same pairs would grade its own homework, so the reported hold-out preservation fits on the even pairs and evaluates on the odd ones. Verdicts are ALIGNED-WITH-MEASURED-LOSS (with neighbour loss and residual attached), INCOMPATIBLE (raw statistics at chance and mapped and raw preservation both at or below the incompatible floor and residual at or above its floor — a conjunction, in the wave's narrow-refutation style), or UNVERIFIABLE, which also carries SAME_FAMILY_REFUSAL_BOUNDARY when the declared pair is two versions of one family: family-level signatures do not support version-level claims, so the probe refuses the question it cannot honestly answer. Different dimensions are INCOMPATIBLE outright — a learned map between them would be a translator, which the tool does not ship by design.

11(i) The fixtures, hazard by hazard

The fixtures (fixtures/, generated deterministically by build_fixtures.py) build two pseudo-embedding models — seeded random projections of a shared 16-topic latent space at the same output dimension (64), plus a third from an unrelated latent corpus — so dimensions match, shape checks pass, and only geometry can tell the spaces apart. No real embedding model is involved; every hazard's correct verdict is known by construction:

  • docs-a (600 vectors) — the clean partition. Written by model A, claiming model A v1.0. Required: CONSISTENT. The audit's known-good arm — a detector that stamps clean data as mixed is manufacturing its market.
  • docs-b (600) — the version lie. Records stamped v0.9 under a manifest claiming v1.0. Required: MIXED / MANIFEST_MISMATCH — the bookkeeping leg catching what geometry cannot: the records and the claim disagree, and the disagreement is itself the evidence.
  • docs-foreign (400) — the quiet case, and the chapter's teaching point. Written entirely by model B; every record claims model A. Its own population is a perfectly consistent single space — the wrong one — so its within-partition screen reads SINGLE-SPACE-CONSISTENT and only the cross-partition signature leg catches it: MIXED / CROSS_PARTITION_SIGNATURE_MISMATCH. This is the reel's failure at partition scale, and the reason the audit has a cross-partition leg at all.
  • docs-mixed (800) — the 5% tranche inside one partition. A model-A partition with 5% model-B vectors mixed in. Required: MIXED / WITHIN_PARTITION_MIXTURE_SIGNAL — the within-population statistic doing the work the cross-partition leg does for docs-foreign.
  • docs-corrupt (400) — truncation, repadded. 30% of vectors truncated to 32 dimensions, then zero-padded back to the declared 64. The repadded vectors keep their geometry on the retained dimensions, so the screen reads clean; the corruption is a bookkeeping catch — MIXED / TRUNCATED_REPADDED_SIGNATURE, from the zero-tail signature.
  • docs-unstamped (300) — no claim at all. No manifest claim, no record provenance. Required: UNVERIFIABLE / UNSTAMPED. Absence of a claim is not evidence of mixture and not evidence of consistency; it is the absence of anything to audit against, stamped as such.
  • The screen populations — clean, 5%, 1%. screen_clean (800 model-A vectors): SINGLE-SPACE-CONSISTENT, frac_neg 0.000. screen_mixed5 (5% model B): SUSPECT-MIXED, frac_neg 0.017. screen_mixed1 (1% model B): the refusal boundary's teaching case — the signal is real (0.002 against a clean 0.000) but inside the registered band, so the screen refuses: UNVERIFIABLE / BELOW_SEPARATION_FLOOR, rather than guess MIXED or stamp a false CONSISTENT.
  • The probe pairs (240 paired texts each). pseudo-A vs pseudo-B — same latent space, different projections: the spaces are genuinely alignable, and the probe must say so with the loss attached (ALIGNED-WITH-MEASURED-LOSS). pseudo-A vs pseudo-C — an unrelated latent corpus: no rotation recovers structure that was never shared (INCOMPATIBLE).
  • The migration fixtures — 500 live ids, 12 tombstones. mig_old holds 500 live documents embedded by model A plus 12 tombstone records; the manifest claims pseudo-B. mig_clean_new re-embeds all 500 by model B: must CERTIFY. mig_broken_new drops 10 live ids (2%) and resurrects 6 tombstoned ones: must REFUSE on completeness — while its retrieval equivalence still passes (0.538) and is reported, because a refusal that hides the passing legs is as dishonest as a certificate that hides the failing one. mig_reel_new is the canonical failure: model-A vectors stamped as pseudo-B — the "completed" migration that never re-embedded. Every provenance record claims the new model; only the equivalence leg can catch it, and it must: REFUSED at overlap 0.008.

11(l) Limitations, named plainly

  • Input formats are the tool's own. v1 reads embedreckon's documented JSONL export and manifest formats only. Direct readers for Qdrant / Weaviate / pgvector exports are future work; any store that can export ids + vectors + provenance fields to JSONL can be audited today, but the conversion is the user's.
  • The screen's floor is a real floor. Contamination in roughly the 1–2% range falls inside the registered refusal band and stamps UNVERIFIABLE by design. A user who needs certainty at 1% contamination needs provenance, not a stronger statistic — the tool says so rather than pretend otherwise.
  • Same-family version swaps are the hard case. The refusal boundary registers it: family-level signatures do not support version-level claims, and two versions of one model family may be geometrically closer than the screen's separation. The probe refuses same-family version questions as inconclusive where the evidence is in between.
  • Alignment is orthogonal only. The probe measures the rotation route and reports its residual honestly. Learned (non-orthogonal) maps are excluded by the dual-use boundary — which is a real capability cost, accepted knowingly: relative-representation and learned-map comparisons exist in the research literature and are not productized here.
  • The detector claim is bounded by the fixture measurement. Kill criterion (i)'s second arm — the same separation test on one real two-model index pair built from public embedding datasets — and criterion (ii)'s arm — three real-world index exports and the fraction arriving with no usable provenance and no constructible golden set — are publication-stage evaluations, not run at build. Until they run, the screen's measured performance is a statement about planted fixtures at fixture scale.
  • Certification needs a golden set, and someone must build it. The equivalence leg is only as representative as the queries and reference results the user recorded from the old index. An unrepresentative golden set certifies an unrepresentative equivalence; the certificate prints the query count and per-query overlaps so the base is visible.
  • No manifest writer. The scan's Position 3 — writing provenance manifests at ingest — is not built; v1 consumes manifests. An index whose pipeline never wrote provenance gets the screen and the probe, not the audit, and the audit's absence is itself reported (UNSTAMPED), not papered over.

Part III — Synthesis

3.1 What the eleven share

Strip the domain nouns off all eleven tools and the same machine remains, five times over with ledgers, eleven times over in spirit:

  • A stamp taxonomy with a narrow REFUTED. Every tool's strongest negative verdict is fenced: deedreckon refutes on NOT_FOUND and VALUE_MISMATCH but not on a 403; edgereckon on three reason codes only; zombieaudit's INFLATED is a conjunction of two thresholds; claimwitness requires the world to positively disagree on re-check. The narrowness is not caution for its own sake — a verification layer whose refutations are themselves unreliable would be worse than none, because it would be believed.
  • Reason-coded refusal as a first-class output. UNVERIFIABLE is never a bare shrug. It decomposes into named evidentiary situations — RATE_LIMITED, NO_GROUND_TRUTH, CALLEE_NOT_EXECUTED, AUTH_CONTEXT_UNAVAILABLE, UNIT_SEMANTICS_NOT_PROBEABLE — each of which tells the consumer what would have to change for the claim to become decidable. A refusal taxonomy is a research agenda: it enumerates, mechanically, what the world would need to provide for its claims to be checkable.
  • Evidence re-read or re-executed, never asserted. effectreckon re-reads state and ignores stdout; claimwitness re-runs the tests; deedreckon re-queries the record; zombieaudit re-aggregates the positions; edgereckon re-executes the suite under trace; quantseal re-measures the file; ontoreckon re-checks every triple against the declared ontology; embedreckon re-measures the index's own vectors and re-computes the golden queries. In no tool does a verdict rest on the claimant's account of the evidence. This is the single invariant, and it is the whole difference between the verification layer and the reporting layer it audits.
  • Tamper-evident records in five of eleven. effectreckon, foriscope, deedreckon, claimwitness, and memreckon write hash-chained append-only ledgers (foriscope additionally HMAC-signs its chain). The pattern is identical in each: canonical JSON, SHA-256 of previous hash plus entry, a verify command that names the first broken link. Verification results are claims too; the chain is how they carry their own evidence.
  • Pre-registered kill criteria. Written in the scans, before the builds: memreckon's 40% checkability / 5% false-REFUTED / 45-day adoption tests; deedreckon's 1% "verification is ceremony" discrepancy line; edgereckon's trace-obtainability and verified-fraction tests; embedreckon's separation test — the screen's false-positive and detection rates measured on planted tranches, re-measured on every validation run. A tool that cannot state the conditions of its own abandonment is a claim about itself that nobody can check.
  • Fixtures with known-correct verdicts. Planted phantom callees, a planted-flaw tournament, one true / one false / one unverifiable claim, four unprobable documented behaviors, a stale symbol beside a refuted test claim. Each tool's "it works" is a checkable statement against constructed ground truth — ground truth about ground truth — and validate.py is the checker. The recursion is deliberate and terminates there: fixtures are simple enough to verify by reading them.

3.2 What the wave proved — and what it did not

Proved: the gaps are measurable, not rhetorical. Before the builds, "agents act on unverified claims" was an argument. After them, it is a set of numbers with denominators. deedreckon, pointed at its author's own operation, refuted 3 of 12 real claims — a production build number for a build that never shipped, an email that was never sent — none flagged by any existing dashboard. zombieaudit's first live run found all five of its INFLATED verdicts fee-driven, up to $2.88M of overstatement on a single wallet, and refused 26 of 50 wallets because the venue's own aggregations of the same record disagree by 2–10×. quantseal showed that a label the entire ecosystem treats as an identity has a 13.3% size spread, a 15% speed spread, and a 52% divergence spread across three publishers — with the rankings disagreeing across metrics, so no relabeling rescues it. edgereckon produced the code-graph category's missing table: one graph at precision 1.000 / recall 0.619, another at 0.727 / 0.381, with every recall miss attributable to a named dynamic mechanism. podiumproof showed a tournament whose champion was 7th of 16 in its own pool, crowned by a judge whose verdicts anti-track quality (beta ≈ −0.09). foriscope's fixture run refused exactly the four unprobable behaviors — no more, no fewer. ontoreckon's fixture audit priced a builder's silence at a 0.353 violation rate over decidable triples and refused the plausible-but-invalid path at its join; embedreckon's screen measured 0/100 false positives on clean populations while refusing the 1% tranche it could not honestly call, and its certifier refused the reel's own scenario — old vectors under new provenance — at a golden-set overlap of 0.008. In each territory, the first measurement was the news.

Not proved: adoption, and several pre-registered legs. No outside user has run any of the eleven; all eleven are published, and none of them has adoption evidence yet. memreckon's kill criterion turns on three real memory stores; only the fixture has been audited. edgereckon's kill criteria were evaluated on the fixture; the real-repo leg — three real open-source codebases — has not run, and the verified-fraction question it asks is genuinely open: a real suite's trace coverage may be far thinner than 31/36 symbols, and the honest output on such a repo may be a wall of UNVERIFIABLE. quantseal's ledger holds one model family's measurements; whether the receipt model scales by demand is untested. deedreckon's dogfood is n=12 from one operation. The wave demonstrated that verification is buildable — pure stdlib, offline, $0, three days — and that its first measurements find real discrepancies everywhere it looks. It did not demonstrate that the ecosystem wants to know. The kill criteria exist precisely so that question gets an honest answer too.

3.3 The compliance and IP pattern

Every tree carries the same three documents beside its code: LICENSE (MIT, all eleven), NAME_CHECK.md, and COMPLIANCE.md. The pattern they implement:

  • Original, collision-checked names. Each tool's name was coined for it and checked on GitHub, PyPI, and npm before use (edgereckon's record: GitHub 0 results, PyPI 404, npm 404, re-verified at build time).
  • Idea only from any shared reference — never name, copy, structure, or code. The scans study competitors the way a patent attorney studies prior art: to locate the gap and to know what not to reproduce. Implementations are clean-room: pure standard library, written from the position statements, not from entrants' sources.
  • License-specific care, per territory. GitNexus is PolyForm Noncommercial — not open source — so no GitNexus code reuse, and its export format is not closely integrated in v1. jCodeMunch: likewise no code reuse. Serena is GPL — no embedding. Graphify's graph format is treated as data processing: reading a JSON export is interoperability, not derivation, and ingest.py's docstring says so in the code. SCIP (Apache-2.0) is identified as the safe interop surface for future graph ingestion. zombieaudit's record carries its own flag: read-only measurement of public data by an author who cannot legally trade on the venue measured. quantseal distributes no weights and names llama.cpp as an external instrument, not a dependency.
  • Design boundaries recorded as compliance. ontoreckon's closed-world reinterpretation of OWL domain/range is recorded in its COMPLIANCE.md as an original design decision, not a representation of OWL semantics; embedreckon's population-only / no-attribution boundary — no translator shipped, no vector attributed to a named model — is recorded the same way, with the dual-use research (Vec2Vec) cited as its reason. RDFLib and pySHACL, though permissively licensed, are not used by ontoreckon: the verdict model is its own. Elasticsearch's tri-license (AGPLv3 / SSPL / Elastic License 2.0) is scan context for embedreckon only — reading exports constrains nothing.
  • Zero third-party dependencies across all eleven trees — which is also a compliance property: there is no dependency license stack to audit, no transitive code in the verification path.
  • Employer-reference scan before any commit — each tree is scanned for employer-identifying material before publication (edgereckon: 0 hits), keeping the public artifacts clean of the author's day-job context.

3.4 Glossary

TermMeaning
verified-or-refuseThe programme's thesis: every claim is stamped VERIFIED, REFUTED, or UNVERIFIABLE (or a domain variant) with a reason code, and downstream answers are computed only over verified evidence; where evidence runs out, the tool refuses rather than fills the gap.
stampA tool's verdict on one claim, recorded with its reason code and evidence. A stamp is a data value a downstream program can branch on, not a sentence in a report.
reason codeA machine-readable token naming the evidentiary basis of a stamp: OBSERVED, NOT_FOUND, RATE_LIMITED, CALLEE_NOT_EXECUTED, NO_GROUND_TRUTH, … The taxonomy of reason codes is the taxonomy of ways a claim can fail to be decidable.
ground truthEvidence obtained by the tool itself from the world — re-read state, a re-run command, a re-aggregated record, an execution trace — as opposed to a claimant's report of that evidence.
decidable edge(edgereckon) A claimed call edge the audit could settle either way: stamped VERIFIED or REFUTED. precision_decidable is computed over decidable edges only.
driftChange in the audited artifact after the claim about it was produced: source files whose hashes differ from a graph's recorded hashes (edgereckon), or a memory whose target artifact is newer than the memory (memreckon's STALE is drift's stamp).
zombie position(zombieaudit) An unsettled position whose market resolved against the holder — current price 0, current value 0, redeemable — a realized economic loss that closed-positions-only win rates never count.
receipt(quantseal) A ledger entry binding one exact file (by SHA-256) to one hardware profile and its measured size, speed, perplexity, KLD, and probe results. Also used for claim audits stamped UNMEASURED.
oracle / headroom / regret(podiumproof) The oracle scores every candidate in the pool against ground truth. Headroom is the premium available — best possible minus typical. Regret is what the tournament left on the table — oracle-best score minus champion score.
hash chainAn append-only log in which each entry embeds the SHA-256 of the previous entry, so any edit, deletion, or reordering breaks every subsequent link at a findable position.
clean-room audit(foriscope) An audit log produced entirely from the tool's own probe records — no model generation anywhere in the chain — HMAC-SHA256-signed and hash-chained so the explanation's evidence can be re-verified independently.
kill criterionA pre-registered, measurable condition under which a tool is abandoned, written in its landscape scan before the build — so the decision to stop cannot be renegotiated by the sunk cost of having built it.
open-world assumption(ontoreckon) OWL's semantic reading: absence of a fact is not its falsity, and domain/range declarations are inference axioms, not constraints — stating that worksFor has domain Person makes an unexpected subject a Person rather than an error. ontoreckon documents its deliberate opposite, a closed-world reading under which the declarations stamp verdicts; every report states which reading its stamps were issued under.
composable join(ontoreckon) A join in a multi-hop path where the range of the incoming relation and the domain of the next share at least one class under the subclass hierarchy — some typing of the join entity could make both hops conformant. Without one, the path is refused NON_COMPOSABLE_JOIN: plausible hop by hop, invalid at the join.
SHACLThe W3C shapes language for validating RDF graphs, and the classical substrate (via pySHACL) that performs conformance checking whenever a human authors the shapes and runs it. An OWL ontology does not automatically become SHACL shapes — the gap between the substrate's existence and its absence from every LLM-era builder pipeline is ontoreckon's territory.
mixed-space contamination(embedreckon) Vectors produced by more than one embedding model — hence living in incompatible vector spaces — stored in one index or partition, typically at identical dimensions so every shape check passes. Detectable as positive evidence per partition (MIXED), or screened at population level under a refusal boundary; never attributable to individual vectors.
Procrustes alignment(embedreckon) The orthogonal (rotation-only) fit that best maps one embedding space onto another over paired examples. The probe reports its residual and the neighbourhood preservation it achieves — on held-out pairs, so the fit cannot grade its own homework — and never an unqualified "compatible": the strongest verdict is ALIGNED-WITH-MEASURED-LOSS, with the loss attached.
golden set(embedreckon) A fixed set of queries, embedded by the new model, with the reference top-k results recorded from the old index — the external evidence against which a migration's retrieval equivalence is measured. It is the one certification leg a consistently falsified provenance record cannot pass, because it compares the index against the world outside its own bookkeeping.

3.6 Applying the pattern in your own systems

The eleven tools are small, but the pattern transfers to systems of any size. If you take one engineering practice from this document into a production agent system, take these five moves, in this order — they are ordered by leverage, and each is cheap relative to the failure it prevents.

Move 1: Separate the verdict path from the action path. effectreckon's _verdict never receives the action record; that is a code-structure decision, not a policy. In your system, the equivalent is: the component that decides "did it work" must not import, read, or be configured by the component that did the work. Concretely — postconditions evaluated against re-read state (a fresh GET, a fresh hash, a fresh query), not against the mutation call's return value. If your success check can be satisfied by the same bug that broke the action, you do not have a success check. Audit your current stack for one question: where does "success" get decided, and what evidence reaches that code? If the answer is "the exit code and the agent's summary," you have located your PocketOS exposure.

Move 2: Make your refusal a designed value, with reasons. Most systems have two outcomes: success and exception. The verification layer needs three verdicts and a reason taxonomy, because the operational responses differ: REFUTED means stop and repair; UNVERIFIABLE/RATE_LIMITED means retry later; UNVERIFIABLE/NO_GROUND_TRUTH means build the missing evidence path (a test suite that runs, a read API that works, a run record at claim time). deedreckon's dogfood shows why the distinction pays: of its three UNVERIFIABLE claims, each had a different correct next action, and none of them was "the claim is false." Design exercise: list your system's current failure strings, and sort them into the world contradicts the claim, we could not reach the world, and the claim was not checkable as made. The second and third buckets are your UNVERIFIABLE taxonomy, and they are usually where the unowned work is hiding.

Move 3: Pre-register your thresholds as named constants. podiumproof's EPS, STABLE, SWAP_GOOD, SWAP_BAD and zombieaudit's tolerance block share a property: the numbers were fixed before the data was seen, they live in one visible place, and they are commented with their meaning. The anti-pattern is the threshold chosen after the run, in a meeting, to make the run look acceptable. If your team evaluates judges, models, or vendors, write the pass marks into the code or the evaluation plan before the evaluation runs, and version them like code. A verdict whose threshold moved is a claim whose evidence was edited.

Move 4: Chain your audit records. The hash chain in these tools is ~30 lines of standard-library Python (read audit.py in any of the five ledger tools). It buys a property that is otherwise expensive: your historical verdicts cannot be quietly edited — by a well-meaning engineer "fixing" a report, by a later process, or by an agent with write access to its own track record. If agents in your system can write to the store that records their performance, the chain (plus an externally held copy of the head hash) is the difference between a record and a narrative. The Replit fabrication — ~4,000 invented records replacing deleted ones — is what an unchained, agent-writable record permits.

Move 5: Build the fixture before you trust the checker. Every tool here ships constructed ground truth: claims whose correct stamps are known because they were planted. This is the move teams skip, because the checker "obviously works" on the happy path. The fixtures are where the sharp edges showed up: foriscope's classifier had to refuse exactly four behaviors; edgereckon's monkey-patch case had to land on UNVERIFIABLE rather than either neighbor; memreckon's STALE had to fire on a claim that was still true. Write the ten cases whose verdicts you are sure of — including at least two that must be refused and one that must be refuted — and make your checker pass them in CI. A verification component without a verdict fixture is itself an unverified claim.

Finally, the meta-lesson of the wave, stated as a budgeting rule: verification was the cheap part. Eleven working verifiers — pure standard library, no model spend, no infrastructure — were built in three days, because checking a claim against re-read evidence is almost always simpler than producing the claim was. The expensive part of the status quo is not the checking; it is the incidents, the mispriced deals, the copied wallets, the broken refactors, and the slow discovery that a dashboard was a narrative. When a team says it cannot afford a verification layer, the eleven chapters above are the counter-ledger: it is already paying for the absence of one, in the currency each territory denominates — deleted volumes, fabricated records, 5%-of-the-time champions, $2.88M of un-subtracted fees, and maps that lose to grep.
